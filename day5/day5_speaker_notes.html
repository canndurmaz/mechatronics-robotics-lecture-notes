Session 3.7 · Resonance, vibration and filter use Curriculum code 3.7 · 60 min (sum 60 min)

Slide 2Agenda and learning objectives~3 min

Welcome to the last day. Today we leave the ideal world of Days 2 to 4: real shafts bend and real sensors are imperfect. Walk through the six sessions and the timing, then read the outcomes in the orange box. Stress that every outcome is something they can explain or calculate with school maths: square roots, logarithms, Ohm's law and simple averages. There is no new heavy theory today; most of it is physical intuition.

Emphasise
  • Today is about the real hardware around the controller.
  • Misconception: "a better controller fixes everything". The hardware sets the limits.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Will we need Laplace transforms today? A: Hardly. Frequency response pictures are enough.

Transition: First, a quick look back.

Slide 3What we learned so far: Days 1–4~4 min

Recap the four days in one minute each: the motor as a torque source, models and PID, sampled control and filters, and the drive with its cascade of current, velocity and position loops. Then explain the three ideas we reuse today in plain words. Frequency response: shake the system with a sine and see how much bigger and how much later the output is. Bandwidth: how fast a command can change and still be followed. Phase margin: how far we are from oscillation. Today both assumptions, rigid load and perfect sensors, fall away.

Emphasise
  • Gain and phase of a sine are the language of today.
  • Misconception: delay only makes things slower. In a loop it reduces stability.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What is bandwidth again? A: Roughly the highest frequency the loop still follows well.

Transition: We start with a swing.

Slide 5Refresher: natural frequency of a mass on a spring~5 min

Ask who has pushed a child on a swing. Push at the right rhythm and the swing goes higher and higher; push at the wrong rhythm and nothing happens. That rhythm is the natural frequency. A mass on a spring behaves the same way, and omega n is the square root of k over m. Work the example: one kilogram on a 100 newton per metre spring gives 10 radians per second, about 1.6 hertz. For rotation, replace mass by inertia J. Resonance means a small push at the natural frequency gives a large motion when damping is low.

Emphasise
  • Stiffer means faster, heavier means slower.
  • Misconception: omega n in rad/s equals f in Hz. Divide by 2 pi.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why does a swing need small pushes only? A: Little damping: energy adds up each cycle.

Transition: Now put that spring between a motor and its load.

Slide 6Why mechanical resonance limits servo bandwidth~4 min

Until now the load was glued rigidly to the motor. In reality couplings, belts and ball screws twist and stretch, so they are springs. Motor inertia, spring and load inertia form a mass spring system with very little damping. The encoder sits on the motor, so the controller only sees the motor side. When we turn the gain up, the controller starts to push the system at its resonance and the axis whistles or rings. Use the car suspension analogy on the slide. Point at the typical frequency ranges: belts are the softest.

Emphasise
  • Resonance, not the controller, usually limits industrial axes.
  • Misconception: steel parts are rigid. At hundreds of hertz they are springs.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why does my machine whistle when I raise the gain? A: The loop is exciting a mechanical resonance.

Transition: Let us calculate where these resonances lie.

Slide 7The two-mass model and its two special frequencies~7 min

Two inertias joined by one spring have two special frequencies. At the anti-resonance the load swings while the motor stands still, so the motor-side sensor sees almost nothing: it depends only on k and J L. At the resonance motor and load swing against each other and the frequency is higher by the square root of one plus R, where R is the inertia ratio. Do the four steps on the board: 1000 rad/s, 159 hertz; R equals 5; 2449 rad/s, 390 hertz. The grey Going further line is optional.

Emphasise
  • Two frequencies: AR from the load alone, R higher by root(1+R).
  • Misconception: there is only one resonance frequency.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What happens to f_AR if the spring is four times stiffer? A: It doubles.

Transition: To read the plots that follow we need decibels.

Slide 8Refresher: decibels (dB)~4 min

Gains in a servo span from thousands to thousandths, so we use a log scale. Decibels are twenty times the log of the gain. Learn the table by heart: times ten is plus 20, times two is about plus 6, one is zero, half is minus 6. The nice property: multiplying gains means adding decibels, so a chain of blocks just adds up on a Bode plot. Example: a 12 dB peak is two times two, so four times larger.

Emphasise
  • 0 dB means unchanged, negative means smaller.
  • Misconception: negative dB means a negative gain. It means a gain below one.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What is -40 dB? A: A gain of 0.01.

Transition: Now we can read the two-mass Bode plot.

Slide 9Frequency response of the two-mass system~6 min

The dashed line is the rigid load: a straight line falling with frequency. The blue curve is the real flexible system. At low frequency they agree. At 159 hertz we see the dip, at 390 hertz a sharp peak, and above that only the motor moves so the gain is six times higher. A larger inertia ratio makes this jump larger and tuning harder. Use the bicycle gear analogy: a gearbox makes the load feel lighter to the motor, by the gear ratio squared.

Emphasise
  • Peak at f_R, dip at f_AR, step of 1+R above.
  • Misconception: the dip is harmless. The load still moves there.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: How does a gear help? A: It divides the reflected load inertia by i squared.

Transition: What does the velocity loop make of this?

Slide 10What the velocity loop sees~6 min

This is the loop gain of a velocity loop tuned for 50 hertz. The dashed curve is the rigid case and crosses 0 dB once. The red curve, with the real mechanics, comes back above 0 dB around 390 hertz: the loop would amplify vibrations there and the axis rings. The blue curve has a notch filter added at 390 hertz: the peak is pushed down to minus 17 dB. The cost is about 9 degrees of phase at the crossover, which is acceptable.

Emphasise
  • Above 0 dB at a resonance means ringing.
  • Misconception: a filter is free. It always costs some phase.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why not just lower the gain? A: Possible, but then the whole axis becomes slower.

Transition: So what exactly is a notch filter?

Slide 11The notch filter: cutting out one frequency~5 min

A notch filter is like a graphic equaliser where you pull down one single slider. It passes everything at 0 dB except a narrow band around its centre frequency, which we place on the resonance. Depth says how much it cuts, width how many hertz it covers. Compare the two curves: the wide notch tolerates a drifting resonance but bends the phase further away from the centre. The biquad formula in grey is for interested students only.

Emphasise
  • Centre on f_R, choose depth and width.
  • Misconception: deeper and wider is always better. It costs phase.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Can I use several notches? A: Yes, one per dominant resonance.

Transition: How do we set one up in practice?

Slide 12Setting up a notch: a practical recipe~4 min

Go through the five steps: measure at low gain, read the peak, set the notch, adjust width, raise the gain and recheck. The rule of thumb is that the notch should be at least three to five times above the loop bandwidth; our example has a ratio of almost eight and costs only 9 degrees. If the resonance is close to the bandwidth, no filter helps: stiffen the mechanics. Mention adaptive notches briefly: they follow a resonance that moves with the payload.

Emphasise
  • Measure first, then filter.
  • Misconception: filters can replace stiff mechanics.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What if f_R moves with the load? A: Use a wider or an adaptive notch.

Transition: The second tool is the low-pass filter.

Slide 13Low-pass torque filters~4 min

A low-pass filter is the digital version of the RC circuit they know: slow signals pass, fast ones are damped. It is the right tool against broad noise, such as encoder steps and current ripple. But look at 390 hertz: a 1 kilohertz low-pass barely reduces it, only 0.6 dB. To cut 390 hertz with a low-pass we would have to put its corner so low that it would ruin the phase at 50 hertz. That is why the notch is the tool for a single resonance.

Emphasise
  • Low-pass for broad noise, notch for one peak.
  • Misconception: a low-pass removes everything above its corner. It only rolls off gradually.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Second order or first order? A: Second order cuts faster but costs more phase.

Transition: Time to check understanding.

Slide 14Check your understanding: resonance~4 min

Give two minutes in pairs, then take answers. Answer 1: f AR equals the square root of k over J L, so doubling J L lowers it by a factor square root of two, about 1.41. Answer 2: a low-pass that damps 390 hertz noticeably would need a corner near or below it and would cost a lot of phase at 50 hertz; the notch cuts only the narrow band. Answer 3: plus 20 dB is a factor of ten.

Emphasise
  • Let students explain in words, not formulas.
  • Misconception: f_AR goes down by a factor of two. It is root two.

Transition: Let us summarise.

Slide 15Summary and key terms: resonance and filters~4 min

Read the summary points and the key terms. The core message: a flexible drive train is a mass spring system, it shows a resonance peak, and the peak can make the loop unstable. Notch for a narrow peak, low-pass for broad noise, but mechanics first. Ask one student to explain the inertia ratio in their own words.

Emphasise
  • Mechanics first, filters second.

Transition: After the break: sensors.

Session 4.1 · Sensors and measurement principles Curriculum code 4.1 · 30 min (sum 30 min)

Slide 17Refresher: accuracy versus precision~7 min

Use the dartboard. Top left: darts close together and on the bull's-eye, accurate and precise. Top right: tight group but off-centre, precise but not accurate; this is a constant offset that calibration can remove. Bottom left: scattered around the centre, accurate on average but not precise; averaging helps. Then define resolution and range. Use the ruler example: fine marks do not help if the ruler is printed in the wrong place.

Emphasise
  • Offset errors hurt accuracy, noise hurts precision.
  • Misconception: more digits on the display means more accurate.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Which error can calibration remove? A: The constant offset, i.e. systematic error.

Transition: First sensor: current.

Slide 18Current measurement: shunt, Hall and fluxgate~7 min

The shunt is pure Ohm's law: a tiny resistor in the current path, and the voltage across it is proportional to the current. Do the example: 2 milliohm times 20 ampere is 40 millivolt, so we need an amplifier, and the shunt dissipates 0.2 watt at 10 ampere. A Hall sensor measures the magnetic field around the conductor instead, so it is electrically isolated from the high voltage. Fluxgate sensors are the precision variant. Isolation means no electrical connection.

Emphasise
  • Shunt: cheap and accurate but not isolated.
  • Misconception: a bigger shunt resistor is better. It wastes power as heat.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why isolation? A: The motor side is at hundreds of volts; the microcontroller must be protected.

Transition: Next: force.

Slide 19Force: strain gauges and load cells~6 min

A strain gauge is a zig-zag foil resistor glued on metal; when the metal stretches the foil gets longer and thinner, so resistance rises. The relative change is about twice the strain, which is tiny. A Wheatstone bridge turns that tiny change into a voltage; with four active gauges we get four times the signal and temperature effects cancel. Example: strain one per mille and 5 volt excitation give 10 millivolt. A load cell is just a metal block with such a bridge.

Emphasise
  • Bridge = sensitivity plus temperature compensation.
  • Misconception: the gauge measures force directly. It measures strain.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why mV/V? A: The output scales with the excitation voltage.

Transition: Now temperature.

Slide 20Temperature: PT100/PT1000, NTC, thermocouple, KTY~5 min

The plot shows resistance on a log scale. Platinum sensors rise almost linearly, about 0.39 percent per kelvin: a PT100 goes from 100 ohm at 0 degrees to about 139 ohm at 100 degrees. NTC thermistors drop steeply and non-linearly: very sensitive but need a table. Thermocouples produce a small voltage from two different metals and suit high temperatures. KTY sensors sit in motor windings to protect against overheating.

Emphasise
  • PT accurate, NTC sensitive, thermocouple hot.
  • Misconception: an NTC is linear. It is strongly non-linear.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Where in a drive is an NTC? A: Usually in the power module and heatsink.

Transition: Let us check understanding.

Slide 21Check your understanding: sensors~3 min

Answers. 1: the readings are tightly grouped, so precise, but about 0.2 kilogram high, so not accurate: an offset that calibration removes. 2: u equals R times i, 1 milliohm times 30 ampere is 30 millivolt; power is R times i squared, 0.001 times 900, which is 0.9 watt. 3: the bridge turns a tiny resistance change into a voltage, four active gauges give four times the signal, and temperature effects cancel.

Emphasise
  • Use units at every step.

Transition: Summary.

Slide 22Summary and key terms: sensors~2 min

Recap the four bullets and the key terms. Ask a student to state the difference between accuracy and resolution in one sentence. Every sensor is specified with this vocabulary.

Emphasise
  • Vocabulary first, then sensor choice.

Transition: Next session: how a drive measures angle.

Session 4.2 · Encoder and resolver Curriculum code 4.2 · 45 min (sum 45 min)

Slide 24Refresher: binary numbers and Gray code~6 min

Write 101 on the board and decode it: four plus zero plus one equals five. N bits give two to the N values. Now count from 3 to 4 in binary: 011 to 100, all three bits change. On a spinning disk the three tracks never switch at exactly the same instant, so for a moment the reader could see 111 or 000: a wild jump. Gray code reorders the values so only one bit changes per step. Show it on the disk.

Emphasise
  • Gray code: one bit per step.
  • Misconception: Gray code gives more resolution. It only avoids false readings.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: How many positions does 12 bit give? A: 4096.

Transition: First encoder type: incremental.

Slide 25Optical incremental encoders~5 min

An LED shines through a disk with slits onto photodiodes, giving square waves A and B, shifted by a quarter period, plus one index pulse Z per revolution. The drive counts pulses, so it only knows how far it moved since switch-on: after power-up it must do a homing run. Example: 2500 lines at 100 revolutions per second gives 250 kilohertz, which the counter hardware must handle.

Emphasise
  • Incremental = relative position.
  • Misconception: the encoder knows where it is after power-up.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What is Z for? A: A reference mark once per revolution, used for homing.

Transition: How do we get direction and four times resolution?

Slide 26Quadrature signals and ×4 counting~5 min

Point at the edges numbered 1 to 11. Each line produces two edges on A and two on B, so counting every edge gives four counts per line. The order of edges tells direction: A before B is forward. Example: 2500 lines times four is 10000 counts, 0.036 degrees each. If both signals change at once a count was missed, which is flagged as an error.

Emphasise
  • 4N counts per revolution.
  • Misconception: N lines give N counts. With x4 decoding it is 4N.

Transition: Absolute encoders avoid homing.

Slide 27Absolute encoders and serial protocols~5 min

The photo shows a Gray-code disk with many tracks: each angle has its own unique code, so the position is known immediately at switch-on. Multi-turn encoders also count whole revolutions. Modern absolute encoders send the position as a digital message with a checksum. Mention the protocol names only as names: SSI, BiSS, EnDat.

Emphasise
  • Absolute: no homing.
  • Misconception: absolute encoders never need a reference. They need it once at commissioning.

Transition: A clever trick for high resolution: sin/cos.

Slide 28Sin/cos encoders and interpolation~5 min

Here the tracks give a sine and a cosine instead of square waves. Plot one against the other and you get a circle; the angle of the arrow is the position within one line, found with atan2. Counting whole periods gives the coarse part. 2048 periods times 4096 steps gives 2 to the 23 counts. If offsets or amplitudes are wrong, the circle becomes an off-centre ellipse and the angle is wrong.

Emphasise
  • Coarse count plus fine angle.
  • Misconception: resolution equals accuracy. Signal errors limit accuracy.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why atan2 and not atan? A: atan2 uses both signs to get the full 360 degrees.

Transition: The robust alternative: the resolver.

Slide 29Resolver: a rotating transformer~5 min

A resolver is a transformer whose coupling depends on angle. The rotor is fed with a sine carrier of several kilohertz; two stator coils at 90 degrees pick up the carrier multiplied by sine and cosine of the angle. The angle sits in the envelopes, the dashed curves. There are no electronics in the motor, so resolvers survive heat and shock. The converter that extracts the angle is in the Going further note.

Emphasise
  • Angle in the envelopes.
  • Misconception: a resolver outputs a digital value. It is purely analogue.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Where are resolvers used? A: Electric vehicles, aerospace, hot environments.

Transition: How do we get speed from all these sensors?

Slide 30Speed from an encoder and the low-speed problem~7 min

Speed is change of position over time. Method M counts pulses in a fixed time; method T times one pulse. Work the example step by step: one count in 125 microseconds with 10000 counts per revolution is 0.8 revolutions per second, 48 rpm. So the speed reading moves in 48 rpm steps! At 60 rpm it jumps between 48 and 96. That is why high-resolution encoders and filtering matter.

Emphasise
  • Speed resolution = one count per sample.
  • Misconception: a speed reading is as smooth as the motion.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: How to improve? A: More counts per revolution, longer window, or filtering.

Transition: Check understanding.

Slide 31Check your understanding: encoders~4 min

Answers. 1: 1000 lines times four is 4000 counts, 360 over 4000 is 0.09 degree per count. 2: in plain binary several bits can change at once; if they do not switch at exactly the same moment the reader sees a wrong value; Gray code changes only one bit. 3: the incremental encoder, because it only counts changes since switch-on; the absolute one reads a unique code.

Emphasise
  • Ask for the reasoning, not just the number.

Transition: Summary.

Slide 32Summary and key terms: encoder and resolver~3 min

Recap and point at the key terms. Ask: which sensor would you choose for a hot, vibrating environment? Answer: resolver. And for the highest resolution? A sin/cos or high-bit absolute encoder.

Emphasise
  • Match the sensor to the environment.

Transition: After the break: sampling.

Session 4.3 · Sampling and digitisation Curriculum code 4.3 · 30 min (sum 30 min)

Slide 34Refresher: sampling and the wagon-wheel effect~6 min

Ask if anyone has seen wheels turn backwards in a film. The camera takes snapshots; if the wheel turns 40 degrees per frame and spokes repeat every 45 degrees, the eye matches each spoke to the one 5 degrees behind, so the wheel seems to turn backwards slowly. A computer sampling a signal has the same problem. Nyquist rule: the signal frequency must be below half the sampling frequency.

Emphasise
  • Sample faster than twice the highest frequency.
  • Misconception: too-fast signals simply disappear. They reappear as slow fake ones.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Is the strobe light the same? A: Yes, a strobe is sampling with light flashes.

Transition: Here is the same effect on a signal.

Slide 35Aliasing in a measured signal~5 min

The thin line is a 900 hertz signal, sampled at 1 kilohertz: the dots. Join the dots and you see a 100 hertz wave, the dashed line. The rule: alias equals the distance to the nearest multiple of the sampling frequency, here 1000 minus 900. Once sampled, nobody can tell this fake 100 hertz from a real one, so no digital filter can remove it.

Emphasise
  • Alias = |f - k fs|.
  • Misconception: a digital filter can clean up aliasing afterwards.

Transition: Next: the amplitude axis, quantisation.

Slide 36Quantisation: turning a voltage into a number~5 min

Sampling chops time; quantisation chops the voltage into steps. An N-bit converter has 2 to the N steps. The staircase on the plot is a 3-bit ADC; the error below stays within plus/minus half a step. Example: a plus minus 50 ampere range with 12 bits gives 24.4 milliampere steps. The SNR rule: about 6 dB per bit, so 74 dB for 12 bits.

Emphasise
  • 6 dB per bit.
  • Misconception: an N-bit ADC really delivers N clean bits. Real ADCs lose 1–2 bits to noise.

Transition: How does an ADC actually find the number?

Slide 37ADC types for drives: SAR and sigma-delta~5 min

The SAR converter plays "guess the number": is the voltage above half? Then above three quarters? One bit per guess, so 12 guesses for 12 bits, fast and with short delay, ideal for phase currents. The sigma-delta converter uses a very fast 1-bit converter and averages, giving many bits but more delay, ideal for load cells and isolated current measurement. Its internal details are Going further.

Emphasise
  • SAR fast, sigma-delta precise.
  • Misconception: more bits is always better. In a loop, delay matters too.

Transition: When should we sample in a drive?

Slide 38Sampling in step with the PWM, and delay~5 min

Recall from Day 4 that PWM switching makes the current ripple. If we sample exactly at the middle of the switching pattern, the valley or peak of the triangle carrier, we hit the average current without ripple. Then the key formula: a delay T d costs 360 times f times T d degrees. Example: 94 microseconds at 1 kilohertz costs 34 degrees, a big piece of the phase margin. Use the shower analogy: delay makes control nervous.

Emphasise
  • Delay costs phase proportional to frequency.
  • Misconception: microseconds do not matter. At kilohertz they do.

Transition: Check understanding.

Slide 39Check your understanding: sampling~2 min

Answers. 1: 9 kilohertz is above 5 kilohertz, it appears at 10 minus 9 equals 1 kilohertz. 2: four extra bits, 6 dB each, about 24 dB, sixteen times better amplitude resolution. 3: after sampling the alias is indistinguishable from a real signal, so the fast content must be removed before the ADC.

Emphasise
  • Link back to the wagon wheel.

Transition: Summary.

Slide 40Summary and key terms: sampling and digitisation~2 min

Recap the five bullets and key terms. Ask a student to explain aliasing with the wagon wheel in one sentence.

Emphasise
  • Nyquist, quantisation, delay.

Transition: After the break: digital filters.

Session 4.4 · Signal processing and filters Curriculum code 4.4 · 90 min (sum 90 min)

Slide 42Filter types at a glance~9 min

Four basic shapes. Low-pass keeps slow signals, high-pass keeps fast ones, band-pass keeps one band, band-stop removes one band, which is our notch. The dashed curves are first order, the solid ones second order: higher order means a steeper edge. Go through the table and ask the audience to name an everyday example: bass and treble knobs on a stereo are low-pass and high-pass filters.

Emphasise
  • Name the filter by what it passes.
  • Misconception: a filter cuts sharply at the corner. It rolls off gradually.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Which one removes a sensor offset? A: High-pass.

Transition: Let us build the simplest filter in software.

Slide 43From an RC low-pass to one line of code~12 min

Draw the RC circuit: the capacitor voltage follows the input with time constant RC. In software we do the same thing sample by sample: move the output a fraction alpha towards the input. Work the four steps: corner 500 hertz gives tau 318 microseconds; sampling at 16 kilohertz gives 62.5 microseconds; alpha is about 0.18; one line of C code. Stress that the filter delays the signal by about tau, which costs phase inside a loop.

Emphasise
  • A low-pass is a delay too.
  • Misconception: alpha is a frequency. It is a dimensionless fraction.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What happens with alpha equals 1? A: No filtering: y follows x immediately.

Transition: Another simple filter: averaging.

Slide 44Moving average: the simplest digital filter~10 min

Average the last N samples, like averaging your last eight quiz scores. The plot shows the gain: it falls and has exact zeros at multiples of fs over N. A disturbance whose period fits the window exactly is cancelled perfectly. Example: 16 samples at 16 kilohertz cancel 1 kilohertz and its harmonics. The price is a delay of half the window and weak damping between the zeros.

Emphasise
  • Exact cancellation of periodic disturbances.
  • Misconception: averaging removes all noise equally.

Transition: These two examples belong to two families: FIR and IIR.

Slide 45FIR versus IIR filters~10 min

The moving average only uses past inputs: that is FIR, finite impulse response. The RC-style filter also reuses its previous output: that is IIR, infinite impulse response, because one impulse echoes forever, fading. Read the table: FIR is always stable but needs many terms and adds delay; IIR needs few terms and little delay, but must be designed carefully. In control loops IIR usually wins. The biquad structure is Going further.

Emphasise
  • FIR: inputs only. IIR: feedback of outputs.
  • Misconception: IIR is unstable. Only if badly designed.

Transition: Two classic IIR designs.

Slide 46Butterworth vs. Bessel: sharp or clean?~9 min

Both are fourth-order low-passes with the same corner. Butterworth cuts more sharply, 38 dB at three times the corner, but its step response overshoots by 11 percent and rings. Bessel cuts less, 25 dB, but the step is clean. Rule: Butterworth when you want to kill noise, Bessel when the waveform shape matters, for example capturing a transient.

Emphasise
  • Sharpness versus waveform fidelity.
  • Misconception: the steepest filter is always best.

Transition: A common source of noise: differentiation.

Slide 47Speed from position: differentiating amplifies noise~12 min

Recall the 48 rpm steps from Session 4.2. The thin line is the raw difference: it jumps between 0, 48 and 96. Differentiation amplifies fast changes, plus 20 dB per decade, and quantisation steps are very fast changes. The orange line is the low-passed signal: smooth, but lagging by about 1.5 milliseconds. The real cure is better resolution or an observer from Day 3.

Emphasise
  • Derivatives amplify noise.
  • Misconception: filtering fixes resolution problems for free.

Transition: And how do we keep aliasing out?

Slide 48Anti-aliasing: analogue before the ADC, digital after~10 min

Back to the wagon wheel: the fix must happen before the camera. An analogue RC filter in front of the ADC damps the noise above fs over 2 so it cannot fold back. If we sample much faster than needed, a simple RC is enough and the sharp filtering is done digitally afterwards. Remember that this analogue filter also adds delay, so keep its corner well above the loop bandwidth.

Emphasise
  • Only analogue filters prevent aliasing.
  • Misconception: oversampling makes the analogue filter unnecessary. It only makes it simpler.

Transition: Check understanding.

Slide 49Check your understanding: filters~10 min

Give time and discuss. Answers. 1: zeros at fs over N equals 1 kilohertz and its multiples, 2, 3 kilohertz and so on. 2: speed is a difference of two rounded counts divided by a tiny sample time, so one count step becomes a large speed jump; differentiation amplifies fast changes. 3: IIR, because it needs few terms and adds little delay, and delay costs phase margin.

Emphasise
  • Discuss why delay matters in loops.

Transition: Summary.

Slide 50Summary and key terms: signal processing and filters~8 min

Go through the summary and the "which filter" table; ask students to cover the right column and guess. Read the key terms at the bottom. Every filter has two faces: what it removes and how much it delays.

Emphasise
  • Gain and delay, always both.

Transition: After the break: communication.

Session 4.5 · Communication and integration + course wrap-up Curriculum code 4.5 · 45 min (sum 45 min)

Slide 52Refresher: what is a network packet?~4 min

Use the letter analogy: an envelope with sender and receiver addresses, the letter itself, and a seal that shows if someone opened it. A packet has a header with addresses, the payload with the actual values, and a CRC to detect damage. Then define bit rate, cycle time, latency and fieldbus. Stress that for machines timing guarantees matter more than raw speed.

Emphasise
  • Header, data, check.
  • Misconception: a faster bit rate always means lower latency.

Transition: Where are packets used in a machine?

Slide 53Communication architecture of a multi-axis machine~4 min

Walk through the picture: the motion controller plans the trajectory and sends set-points over EtherCAT to the drives; each drive talks to its own encoder; the factory IT sits above. The table shows three layers with very different cycle times, from tens of microseconds inside the drive to seconds for factory data.

Emphasise
  • Fast inside the drive, slower outside.

Transition: The classic fieldbus: CAN.

Slide 54CAN and CANopen~4 min

CAN is two twisted wires shared by all nodes. If two nodes start at once, the message with the lower identifier wins without collision, like a polite queue by priority. Work the example: about 120 microseconds per frame, nine frames per cycle, 1.1 milliseconds. So CAN is fine for slow or few axes, not for fast multi-axis motion.

Emphasise
  • Priority arbitration, ≥1 ms cycles.
  • Misconception: messages collide and are lost on CAN. Arbitration prevents it.

Transition: How do drives understand each other?

Slide 55CiA 402: a common language for drives~4 min

CiA 402 is a drive profile: every vendor's drive accepts the same commands. Use the car analogy for the state machine: unlocked, ignition, engine on, driving; a fault drops you back. The controller writes a control word and reads a status word. In cyclic synchronous modes the controller sends a new set-point each cycle. Object numbers and the full state machine are Going further.

Emphasise
  • One language, many vendors.

Transition: The fast bus: EtherCAT.

Slide 56EtherCAT: processing on the fly~5 min

Use the train analogy: one frame travels through all drives; each drive unloads its set-points and loads its measurements as the train passes without stopping. The working counter tells the master everybody answered. Example: 32 axes fit in about 65 microseconds, so a 100 microsecond cycle is possible. Distributed clocks mean all drives act at the same instant.

Emphasise
  • One frame for all drives.

Transition: How do the buses compare?

Slide 57Comparing interfaces and fieldbuses~3 min

Read the table row by row and connect it to what they now know: SPI on the board, RS-485 for encoders, CAN for slow robust tasks, EtherCAT and PROFINET for fast motion. Choose by cycle time and synchronisation, not bit rate alone.

Emphasise
  • Cycle time and sync decide.

Transition: Why does cycle time matter so much?

Slide 58Latency budget: which loops may run over the bus?~5 min

The data takes two to three bus cycles to go round. Use the same formula as before: 625 microseconds at 30 hertz costs 7 degrees, fine; at 200 hertz it costs 45 degrees, not acceptable. So fast loops stay inside the drive and only position set-points travel over the bus. Shower analogy: a long pipe makes temperature control slow.

Emphasise
  • Fast loops local.
  • Misconception: the bus is fast enough for everything.

Transition: Last point: can we trust the data?

Slide 59Is the data valid? CRC, counters, watchdogs, timestamps~4 min

A correct CRC only proves the bits were not damaged; the value might still be stale. Counters reveal lost or frozen packets, watchdogs trip when nothing new arrives, timestamps give the age, and plausibility checks catch impossible values. Follow the flowchart: any failed check leads to a safe stop.

Emphasise
  • Correct is not the same as fresh.

Slide 60Check your understanding: communication~3 min

Answers. 1: header with addresses, payload with the values, CRC to detect damage. 2: 360 times 100 times 0.001 equals 36 degrees, too much; close that loop in the drive. 3: a sequence counter, a timestamp or a watchdog; the CRC cannot detect old data.

Emphasise
  • Reuse the phase formula.

Session 3.7 · Resonance, vibration and filter use Curriculum code 3.7 · 60 min

Slide 2Agenda and learning objectives~4 min

Welcome to the final day. Over the previous four days we built a servo axis from the inside out: the machine, its model, the control theory, and the drive with its cascaded loops. Today we close the loop with the real world. We start with one hour on mechanical resonance and the filters that tame it, which is the last piece of servo tuning. Then four hours on sensing: what we measure, how encoders and resolvers work, how signals are sampled and digitised, how we filter them, and how drives communicate and synchronise on a fieldbus. We finish with a wrap-up of the whole course. Point at the objectives box: by the end of the day each participant should be able to estimate resonance frequencies, size a notch, estimate velocity quantisation noise and budget latency on a bus.

Emphasise
  • Today connects mechanics and measurement to the control theory of Days 2 to 4.
  • Breaks are extra to the 5 h net time.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Will there be exercises? A: No lab, but several worked numerical examples we compute together.

Transition: Let us begin with the reason why real axes never behave like a pure inertia.

Slide 4Why mechanical resonance limits servo bandwidth~3 min

Until now we assumed the load is rigidly attached to the motor, so the plant is one over J total times s. In reality every coupling, shaft, belt and ball screw is a spring. A spring between two inertias forms a lightly damped mode, typically with damping ratios of one to ten percent. The velocity loop sees this mode through the motor encoder as a gain peak at the resonance frequency and a dip at the anti-resonance. When we raise the gain to make the axis stiffer, the peak eventually crosses zero dB and the axis rings or becomes unstable. Note the typical frequency ranges in the box: belts are the softest, couplings the stiffest. Because the encoder sits on the motor, the feedback is collocated, and that is why a notch filter is usually effective.

Emphasise
  • Resonance, not the controller, is the usual bandwidth limit in industrial axes.
  • Collocated feedback keeps the phase well behaved.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why does the machine whistle when I increase gain? A: The loop gain at a mechanical resonance has exceeded unity; the oscillation frequency is the resonance.

Transition: To quantify this we need a model: the two-mass system.

Slide 5Two-mass model: equations of motion~5 min

Here is the classical two-mass model. The motor inertia J_M and the load inertia J_L are connected by a torsional spring k and a damper c. The shaft torque T_S is the spring torque plus the damping torque. Writing Newton's law for both inertias and taking the Laplace transform with zero load torque gives the two transfer functions on the right. Both share the denominator Delta of s. The motor-side transfer function G_M has a pair of zeros, the anti-resonance, and a pair of poles, the resonance. The load-side transfer function G_L has only the poles, so its phase drops to minus 270 degrees: that is the non-collocated case, much harder to control. Note the asymptotes: at low frequency the system is one rigid body, at high frequency only the motor moves. With a gearbox, reflect J_L and k through the square of the ratio.

Emphasise
  • G_M: zeros then poles. G_L: poles only.
  • Gear ratio reflects inertia and stiffness by 1/i squared.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why not measure on the load? A: Load-side feedback is non-collocated; it gives better accuracy but limits bandwidth. Dual-loop schemes combine both.

Transition: From these equations we can read the two characteristic frequencies directly.

Slide 6Resonance and anti-resonance frequencies~6 min

The numerator of G_M vanishes at omega AR equal to root of k over J_L: the load oscillates against a motor that appears clamped. The denominator gives omega R equal to root of k times the sum of inertias over their product: motor and load swing in anti-phase. The ratio of the two is root of one plus R, where R is the inertia ratio. Let us compute the example together: J_M is 1.2 times ten to the minus four, J_L five times larger, k is 600 newton metres per radian. Omega AR is 1000 radians per second, 159 hertz. Omega R is 1000 times root six, 2449 radians per second, 390 hertz. The gain step between the asymptotes is 20 log six, 15.6 dB. So above 390 hertz the plant looks six times lighter than the controller expects.

Emphasise
  • omega_AR = sqrt(k/J_L); omega_R = omega_AR sqrt(1+R).
  • Gain step 20 log(1+R) dB.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Which frequency is visible in a velocity-loop oscillation? A: Usually the resonance f_R, where the motor-side gain peaks.

Transition: Let us see what that looks like in a Bode plot.

Slide 7Frequency response of the two-mass system~5 min

This is G_M computed with the numbers from the previous slide. The dashed line is the rigid model one over J total s. The thin dotted line is the motor-alone asymptote, 15.6 dB higher. The real curve follows the rigid line at low frequency, dips sharply at 159 hertz where the load absorbs the motor torque, then peaks at 390 hertz and finally follows the motor-only line. The height of the peak and the depth of the dip depend on damping. In metal drive trains damping is only a few percent, so the peak can be twenty dB or more above the asymptote. Remember this picture: you will see it whenever you measure a plant FRF on a real machine.

Emphasise
  • Dip first (AR), then peak (R) on the motor side.
  • Peak height set by damping.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Can the dip be above the peak? A: No, for G_M the anti-resonance is always below the resonance.

Transition: How does the picture change with the inertia ratio?

Slide 8Effect of the inertia ratio R = J_L/J_M~5 min

Here J_M and k are fixed and we vary the load. With R equal to one the two frequencies are close and the step between asymptotes is only six dB. With R equal to ten the anti-resonance moves down, the gap widens by root eleven and the step is 21 dB. The rigid-body gain falls with one over one plus R, but the high-frequency gain does not change, so a controller tuned for the total inertia has far too much gain above the resonance. That is the practical reason for inertia-ratio rules of thumb: below three to five for very stiff, high-bandwidth axes, below ten for general servo work. A gearbox reduces the reflected ratio by the square of the gear ratio; the inertia-matching ratio root of J_L over J_M maximises load acceleration.

Emphasise
  • Large R = wide separation and large gain step.
  • Gear ratio is the strongest lever on R.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Is inertia matching always optimal? A: It maximises acceleration for a given motor; stiffness, speed limits and cost often lead to other choices.

Transition: Now let us put this plant into the velocity loop.

Slide 9Effect on the velocity-loop open-loop FRF~7 min

This is the open loop of a PI velocity controller tuned for 50 hertz crossover on the rigid model, including a two kilohertz current loop and 125 microseconds of delay. The dashed rigid curve crosses zero dB once, with 75 degrees phase margin. With the two-mass plant, shown in red, the resonance lifts the loop gain to plus 12.6 dB at 390 hertz, creating additional zero-dB crossings: the axis will ring or go unstable. The blue curve adds a notch at 390 hertz with zeta z 0.02 and zeta p 0.6. The peak is now minus 17 dB, the gain margin is about 9 dB, and the price is about 8.6 degrees of phase at crossover, phase margin drops from 75 to 66.5 degrees. This is the typical trade-off.

Emphasise
  • Resonance creates extra 0 dB crossings.
  • Notch restores gain margin at a small phase cost.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why not just reduce the gain? A: We could, but crossover would have to drop well below f_AR, sacrificing stiffness.

Transition: Let us look at the notch filter itself.

Slide 10The biquad notch filter~6 min

The notch is a biquad with identical natural frequency in numerator and denominator but different damping. Three parameters: the centre frequency omega n, which we place on the measured resonance; the depth, which is the ratio zeta z over zeta p at omega n; and the width, which for a deep notch is approximately two zeta p omega n, the minus three dB band. The filter has unity gain far below and far above, phase lag below the centre and phase lead above. The approximate lag at crossover is shown at the bottom. On the right is the digital implementation using the Tustin transform with pre-warping, so that the notch lands exactly at omega n despite frequency warping. These formulas are exactly what drive firmware uses.

Emphasise
  • Depth = zeta_z/zeta_p; width approx 2 zeta_p omega_n.
  • Pre-warp when discretising.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What happens without pre-warping? A: The notch shifts to a lower frequency, noticeably when f_n approaches a tenth of f_s or more.

Transition: The Bode plot makes the role of each parameter visible.

Slide 11Notch filter: width and depth in the Bode plot~4 min

Four variants centred at 400 hertz. With zeta z zero the notch is infinitely deep and the phase jumps by 180 degrees. Increasing zeta z to 0.03 or 0.1 limits the depth to minus 20 or minus 9.5 dB. Reducing zeta p to 0.1 makes the notch narrow: little phase cost at low frequency, but it must hit the resonance precisely, and resonances drift with payload, position and temperature. A wide notch is robust against that drift but costs more phase at crossover. In practice, choose only as deep as necessary and as wide as the uncertainty of the resonance frequency demands.

Emphasise
  • Deep and wide costs phase; narrow requires accuracy.
  • Size depth to the peak, width to the drift.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Should depth always be infinite? A: No; extra depth adds phase change without benefit once the peak is below the target line.

Transition: Here is a procedure to set these parameters on a real axis.

Slide 12Selecting notch parameters: a practical procedure~5 min

First measure the FRF at low gain, using the identification methods of Day 2. Read the resonance and check how much it moves over the stroke and payload range. Set the centre on the resonance, the depth to the peak height plus six to ten dB of margin, and the width according to the drift. Re-measure and verify phase margin at crossover and gain margin at the resonance. Then increase the velocity gain until the next limit appears. The box computes the phase cost for our example: about minus 8.6 degrees, matching the plot two slides back. The rule of thumb: keep the notch at least three to five times above crossover. If it is closer, the mechanics need to be stiffer.

Emphasise
  • Measure, place, verify, repeat.
  • f_n at least 3 to 5 times f_c.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: How many notches can be used? A: Drives typically offer two to four; each one costs phase, so fix the mechanics first.

Transition: Notches handle narrow peaks; for broad high-frequency content we use low-pass filters.

Slide 13Low-pass torque (current-reference) filters~4 min

The torque reference filter is usually a first- or second-order low pass. It suppresses broad-band high-frequency content: many small modes above five times crossover, encoder quantisation noise amplified by the velocity gain, and current ripple. Its cost is phase lag everywhere below its corner. With crossover at 100 hertz and a 1 kilohertz corner, the first-order filter costs 5.7 degrees, the second-order 8.1 degrees. But look at the attenuation at our 390 hertz resonance: only 0.6 dB. A low pass simply cannot remove a resonance close to crossover; a notch can. Typical corner: five to ten times crossover.

Emphasise
  • LPF for broad high-frequency content, notch for narrow peaks.
  • Phase lag at crossover is the cost.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: First or second order? A: Second order attenuates noise more strongly above the corner for similar lag at crossover.

Transition: What if the resonance frequency moves during operation?

Slide 14Adaptive notch: tracking a moving resonance~4 min

When the resonance changes with payload, axis position on a long screw, or belt tension, a fixed notch must be very wide. An adaptive notch estimates the dominant frequency online, from the torque reference or the velocity error, using an FFT peak search, an adaptive IIR filter or a gradient update of the notch frequency, and moves the notch accordingly. Adaptation must be supervised: freeze it when the signal is small, bound the frequency range, and beware of adapting to a forced disturbance such as a cutting frequency, which is not a resonance at all. Many commercial drives offer such a function; understand its limits before enabling it.

Emphasise
  • Adaptation needs bounds and supervision.
  • Distinguish resonance from forced vibration.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Can it make things worse? A: Yes, if it locks onto a forced frequency or interacts with another notch.

Transition: Let me summarise the resonance session.

Slide 15Summary: resonance and filter use~2 min

Compliance turns the simple inertia into the two-mass transfer function with an anti-resonance and a resonance root one plus R higher. High inertia ratios make tuning harder. Notches remove narrow peaks cheaply if they are well above crossover; low-pass filters handle broad high-frequency content. And the design order on the right: stiffen mechanics and reduce R first, measure, filter, retune, verify over the whole operating envelope.

Emphasise
  • Mechanics first, filters second.

Transition: After a short break we turn to sensors and measurement principles.

Session 4.1 · Sensors and measurement principles Curriculum code 4.1 · 30 min

Slide 17Metrology vocabulary~7 min

Before discussing specific sensors, we need precise vocabulary. The four targets illustrate accuracy versus precision: accuracy is closeness to the true value and contains systematic and random error; precision is the spread of repeated readings. Repeatability is precision under identical short-term conditions. Resolution is the smallest detectable change, for example one count or one LSB. Range is the span. Linearity is the maximum deviation from a best-fit line in percent of full scale. The key warning in the note: resolution is not accuracy. A 23-bit encoder resolves 0.15 arc seconds, but its absolute error may be twenty arc seconds due to disk eccentricity and mounting.

Emphasise
  • Resolution is not accuracy.
  • Datasheets use these terms differently: read the definitions.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Which matters for a velocity loop? A: Resolution and noise; absolute accuracy matters for positioning.

Transition: Let us apply this vocabulary to current sensors.

Slide 18Current measurement: shunt, Hall and fluxgate~8 min

The shunt is a small resistor in the phase or leg; its voltage is R times i. It is cheap, accurate and fast, but not isolated, so it needs an isolated amplifier or an isolated sigma-delta modulator. The example: 2 milliohm at 20 amps gives 40 millivolts and 0.2 watts at 10 amps RMS. Hall sensors measure the flux in a slotted core; open-loop versions drift, closed-loop versions null the flux with a compensation winding and reach about half a percent. Fluxgates detect zero flux with a periodically saturated core and have extremely low offset. Point out the last bullet: offset in the phase currents produces torque ripple at the electrical frequency, gain mismatch at twice the electrical frequency.

Emphasise
  • Shunt: accurate but needs isolation.
  • Offset gives 1x ripple, gain mismatch 2x ripple.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why are shunts popular in servo drives? A: Low cost, high bandwidth and excellent linearity once isolated sigma-delta modulators are available.

Transition: Next, force sensing with strain gauges.

Slide 19Force: strain gauges and load cells~6 min

A strain gauge is a foil resistor whose resistance changes with strain; the gauge factor is about two. Strains are in the micro-strain range, so the resistance change is tiny and we use a Wheatstone bridge. With four active arms, the full bridge, sensitivity is four times that of a single gauge, temperature effects cancel and bending can be rejected. Example: one thousand micro-strain, gauge factor two, five volts excitation gives ten millivolts. Load cells are specified in millivolts per volt, typically two at nominal load, so the signal chain needs a low-noise instrumentation amplifier or a 24-bit sigma-delta ADC. The photos show a bare foil gauge and an industrial compression load cell.

Emphasise
  • Bridge output is millivolts: noise and drift dominate.
  • Full bridge for sensitivity and compensation.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What is ratiometric measurement? A: Using the excitation as ADC reference so that excitation drift cancels.

Transition: Temperature sensors protect the motor and the inverter.

Slide 20Temperature: NTC, PT100/PT1000, thermocouple, KTY~7 min

The plot shows a PT1000 and a 10 kilo-ohm NTC on a log scale. Platinum sensors follow the Callendar-Van Dusen equation and are nearly linear and accurate; class A tolerance is 0.15 plus 0.002 times temperature. NTCs are very sensitive but strongly non-linear; they sit in IGBT modules and on heatsinks. Thermocouples generate a Seebeck voltage of about 41 microvolts per kelvin for type K and need cold-junction compensation; they are used at high temperature. KTY silicon sensors, about one kilo-ohm at 100 degrees, are common in motor windings and feed thermal models, while PTC thermistor triplets are only used for a trip signal.

Emphasise
  • PT accurate, NTC sensitive, thermocouple hot, KTY in motors.
  • Winding sensors lag the copper: thermal models fill the gap.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why 3- or 4-wire PT connections? A: To cancel the lead resistance, which would otherwise appear as a temperature offset.

Transition: A quick summary before encoders.

Slide 21Summary: sensors and measurement principles~2 min

Specify sensors with the full vocabulary. For currents choose between shunt, Hall and fluxgate based on isolation, accuracy and cost. Force sensing delivers millivolts per volt, so the amplifier matters. Temperature sensors are chosen by accuracy, sensitivity and range. The checklist on the right applies to every sensor you select.

Emphasise
  • Ask which error matters: offset, gain, noise or drift.

Transition: Now the most important sensor of a servo: the position sensor.

Session 4.2 · Encoder and resolver Curriculum code 4.2 · 45 min

Slide 23Optical incremental encoders~4 min

The photo shows a slotted code disk and an optical fork. Light passes through the disk lines and phase-shifted gratings onto photodiodes, producing two signals A and B shifted by 90 degrees plus an index Z once per revolution, usually as RS-422 differential lines. Counting edges gives position relative to power-up, so an incremental axis must be homed. Note the frequency: 2500 lines at 6000 rpm is 250 kilohertz per channel and one million edges per second after decoding. Magnetic and inductive encoders work on the same principle with lower resolution but higher robustness.

Emphasise
  • Incremental means homing.
  • Check maximum output frequency at top speed.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Is Z needed? A: It gives a repeatable reference for homing and for checking lost counts.

Transition: How do we get direction and four times the resolution?

Slide 24Quadrature signals and x4 decoding~6 min

Here are A, B and Z. One line corresponds to one full period of A. Every edge of A and of B is counted, so we get four counts per line: x4 decoding. The table is the state machine: the sequence 00, 10, 11, 01 counts up, the reverse counts down. If both bits change at once, we missed an edge, which is an error. Direction is given by which channel leads. In hardware, a glitch filter rejects short pulses, but it also limits the maximum edge rate, so filter settings and maximum speed must be consistent.

Emphasise
  • 4N counts per revolution.
  • Both bits changing = lost count.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Can x4 decoding cause errors? A: Only if signals have poor 90-degree phasing; then counts are unevenly spaced.

Transition: Absolute encoders avoid homing altogether.

Slide 25Absolute encoders, Gray code and serial protocols~6 min

An absolute encoder delivers a unique code for every angle. Single-turn resolutions of 23 bits, over eight million positions, are common. Multi-turn versions add a revolution counter with gears, battery backup or Wiegand energy harvesting. Internally, Gray code is used: only one bit changes per step, so a reading at a transition is never wildly wrong. The small disk shows a three-bit Gray code, the photo a real 13-track encoder. Positions are transmitted serially: SSI is simple and unidirectional, BiSS-C is open and bidirectional with CRC, EnDat 2.2 adds diagnostics and electronic ID, and one-cable solutions put the link into the motor power cable.

Emphasise
  • Gray code avoids false codes.
  • Serial protocols carry CRC and diagnostics.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why not plain binary on the disk? A: Multiple bits would change at once and misaligned tracks would give huge errors.

Transition: High resolution often comes from interpolating analogue signals.

Slide 26Sin/cos encoders and interpolation~6 min

Sin/cos encoders deliver analogue sine and cosine signals, typically one volt peak-to-peak, with a few hundred to a few thousand periods per revolution. The coarse position counts periods; the fine position inside a period is atan2 of the two signals. With 2048 periods and 12-bit interpolation we reach 23 bits. The Lissajous plot shows the ideal circle and a distorted ellipse. Offset moves the centre and causes an error once per signal period; amplitude mismatch and phase error distort the ellipse and cause twice-per-period errors. Drives correct this by online calibration. Ultimately signal quality and ADC noise limit interpolation, not arithmetic.

Emphasise
  • atan2 interpolation.
  • Offset -> 1st harmonic, gain/phase -> 2nd harmonic.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Will these errors show in the velocity loop? A: Yes, as velocity ripple at a frequency proportional to speed.

Transition: The resolver uses the same sine and cosine idea, but with a transformer.

Slide 27Resolver: principle and signals~5 min

A resolver is a rotary transformer. The rotor winding is excited with a carrier of five to twenty kilohertz; two stator windings ninety degrees apart pick up the carrier modulated by the sine and cosine of the angle. The plots show it: the excitation, then the two output signals whose envelopes, dashed, carry the angle. For illustration the carrier is slowed down. Because there are no electronics in the motor, resolvers tolerate high temperatures, shock and vibration, which is why they dominate in automotive and harsh environments. Accuracy is typically five to ten arc minutes, lower than optical encoders.

Emphasise
  • Angle is in the envelopes.
  • Robust but less accurate.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What is a multi-speed resolver? A: A resolver with several pole pairs; more electrical cycles per revolution improve resolution but lose single-turn absoluteness.

Transition: How do we extract the angle from these signals?

Slide 28Resolver-to-digital conversion: the tracking loop~5 min

The standard method is a tracking loop. Multiply the sine signal by cosine of the estimated angle and the cosine signal by sine of the estimate, subtract, and demodulate synchronously with the carrier. The trigonometric identity gives sine of the angle error, which for small errors is the error itself. A PI controller and an integrator form a type-II loop that drives the error to zero; the integrator output is the angle estimate, and its input is a clean velocity estimate as a bonus. Zero steady-state error at constant speed; under acceleration there is a lag proportional to acceleration. The loop bandwidth trades noise against lag.

Emphasise
  • RDC is a type-II PLL.
  • Velocity comes for free from the loop.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Hardware or software RDC? A: Both are common; software RDC in the drive MCU saves cost and allows tuning.

Transition: From position to velocity: the next challenge.

Slide 29Velocity from position: M-, T- and M/T-method~5 min

Three classical methods. The M-method counts the edges in a fixed sample period: good at high speed, but at low speed few counts mean coarse resolution. The T-method measures the time between two edges with a fast clock: excellent at low speed, but at high speed only few clock cycles are counted, and if no edge arrives there is no update. The M/T method counts edges and clock cycles over a window that starts and ends on an edge, giving good accuracy over the whole range. Modern drives use edge time-stamping combined with high-resolution absolute position, which is essentially the M/T idea in hardware.

Emphasise
  • M at high speed, T at low speed, M/T everywhere.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why not always use T? A: At high speed the clock count between edges becomes small and the update stalls at standstill.

Transition: Let us quantify the low-speed problem.

Slide 30Low-speed limits and quantisation noise~6 min

The plot shows the relative quantisation error of the three methods for 2500 lines, 125 microseconds and a 100 megahertz clock. Compute the M-method resolution with the audience: 60 divided by 4 N T_s is 48 rpm. At 60 rpm the raw estimate jumps between 48 and 96! Treating position quantisation as uniform noise, the difference gives a velocity noise standard deviation of root two times q over root twelve over T_s, about 20 rpm. With a 23-bit absolute encoder the step drops to 0.057 rpm. This is why high-resolution encoders matter for smooth low-speed motion.

Emphasise
  • Delta n = 60/(4 N T_s) = 48 rpm here.
  • Resolution, not tuning, limits low-speed smoothness.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Can we sample slower to improve resolution? A: Yes, but that adds delay to the velocity loop; observers are the better compromise.

Transition: Summary of the encoder session.

Slide 31Summary: encoder and resolver~2 min

Incremental encoders are simple and fast but need homing. Absolute encoders use Gray code and serial protocols with CRC. Sin/cos encoders interpolate with atan2 and suffer harmonic errors from offset and gain. Resolvers are robust and use a tracking loop. Velocity estimation at low speed is a quantisation problem. The table compares the three families.

Emphasise
  • Choose the sensor by resolution, robustness and diagnostics.

Transition: Next we look at how analogue signals become numbers.

Session 4.3 · Sampling and digitisation Curriculum code 4.3 · 30 min

Slide 33ADC architectures for drives: SAR and sigma-delta~7 min

Two converter families dominate. The SAR converter samples and holds, then performs a binary search with a comparator and an internal DAC, needing N steps. It has a sharp sampling instant and low latency, which is ideal for phase currents; most MCU ADCs are SAR. The sigma-delta converter oversamples with a one-bit modulator and noise shaping, followed by a digital sinc decimation filter. It achieves 16 to 24 bits, but the filter introduces group delay and the sample represents an average over a window. Isolated sigma-delta modulators placed next to the shunt, with the sinc filter in the MCU or FPGA, are now standard for high-performance current measurement.

Emphasise
  • SAR: low latency. Sigma-delta: resolution and isolation.
  • Decimation filter delay belongs in the loop model.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why is the sinc filter order often three? A: A sinc-cubed filter matches a second-order modulator and suppresses its shaped noise sufficiently.

Transition: What resolution do we actually get?

Slide 34Quantisation and SNR = 6.02N + 1.76 dB~7 min

The plot shows a sine quantised with three bits and the quantisation error, which looks like a sawtooth bounded by half an LSB. Modelling it as uniform noise gives power q squared over twelve. A full-scale sine has power two to the 2N times q squared over eight. The ratio is 1.5 times two to the 2N, which is 6.02 N plus 1.76 dB. Twelve bits give 74 dB, sixteen bits 98 dB. Real converters fall short; the effective number of bits, ENOB, is computed from the measured SINAD. Example: plus minus 50 amps with 12 bits gives an LSB of 24.4 milliamps. Oversampling and averaging gain 10 log M dB if the noise is white.

Emphasise
  • SNR = 6.02N + 1.76 dB for a full-scale sine.
  • Use ENOB, not N, for design.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Does averaging always help? A: Only if the noise is uncorrelated and covers several LSBs; otherwise a dither is needed.

Transition: Sampling also has a frequency-domain trap: aliasing.

Slide 35Aliasing and the Nyquist criterion~7 min

A 900 hertz sine sampled at one kilohertz: the samples, the dots, lie exactly on a 100 hertz sine. After sampling, the two are indistinguishable. In general the alias appears at the absolute value of f minus k times f_s. The spectrum below shows the folding about half the sampling frequency. Nyquist says a signal is uniquely represented only if its highest frequency is below f_s over two. In drives, PWM ripple, encoder interpolation errors or a mechanical resonance can alias into the velocity loop and appear as a slow, mysterious oscillation. The crucial point: once aliased, no digital filter can remove it.

Emphasise
  • Alias = |f - k f_s|.
  • Prevent aliasing before the sampler.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Can a 1 kHz loop see a 1.05 kHz vibration? A: Yes, as a 50 Hz alias.

Transition: Drives avoid PWM aliasing structurally by synchronous sampling.

Slide 36Synchronous sampling with PWM, timestamps and latency~7 min

With centre-aligned PWM, the phase current ripple is symmetric around the carrier peak and valley. Sampling exactly there returns the average current, without ripple and far from switching edges, so no analogue filter is needed for the ripple. We can sample once or twice per PWM period. All measurements, current, encoder and bus data, should be time-stamped on a common time base. The bottom bar shows the timing: sample, convert, compute, and the new duty becomes active in the next period, giving an effective delay of about 1.5 sample periods. At 16 kilohertz that is 94 microseconds, which costs 34 degrees at one kilohertz. Remember also the rule: the phase loss is 360 times f_c times T_d, so f_s around ten to twenty times crossover.

Emphasise
  • Sample at carrier peak/valley.
  • Delay to phase: 360 f T_d degrees.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: How do we reduce the 1.5 T_s delay? A: Sample late, compute fast and update immediately, or use double update.

Transition: Summary of sampling.

Slide 37Summary: sampling and digitisation~2 min

SAR for latency, sigma-delta for resolution and isolation. Ideal SNR is 6.02 N plus 1.76 dB. Anything above half the sampling frequency folds back, so filter before sampling. Sample synchronously with PWM and time-stamp. And every sample of delay is phase lag. The key message on the right: the measurement chain sets the achievable bandwidth.

Emphasise
  • Measurement chain limits bandwidth.

Transition: After a break we move to signal processing and filters, our longest session.

Session 4.4 · Signal processing and filters Curriculum code 4.4 · 90 min

Slide 39Filter types at a glance~8 min

Four basic filter types, plotted as real curves. Low-pass and high-pass are shown in first and second order; band-pass with quality factor two; band-stop as a notch. The table gives the second-order prototypes and typical drive uses: low-pass for noise and anti-aliasing, high-pass to remove offsets or as wash-out in feed-forward, band-pass to extract a known frequency, for example in vibration monitoring or resolver demodulation, and band-stop for resonances and ripple. Remind the audience of Day 3, where these filters were introduced in continuous time; today we implement them digitally.

Emphasise
  • Same denominator, different numerators.
  • Every type has a drive application.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What is the difference between band-stop and notch? A: A notch is a narrow band-stop, usually of second order.

Transition: We start with the simplest digital filter.

Slide 40First-order low-pass: from H(s) to a difference equation~9 min

The first-order low-pass discretised exactly gives the well-known exponential smoothing: y of k equals y of k minus one plus alpha times the difference between input and previous output, with alpha equal to one minus e to the minus omega f T_s. Tustin gives a slightly different form that matches the frequency response better near half the sampling rate. The complementary high-pass is simply input minus low-pass output. Work the example: 500 hertz corner at 16 kilohertz gives omega f T_s 0.196 and alpha 0.178. The low-frequency delay is one over omega f, 318 microseconds, about five samples. Emphasise the note: every low-pass in a loop is a delay and must appear in the loop model.

Emphasise
  • alpha = 1 - exp(-omega_f T_s).
  • LPF = delay of about 1/omega_f.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Is alpha = omega_f T_s good enough? A: Only for small omega_f T_s; at 0.2 the error is about 10 percent.

Transition: The moving average is the simplest FIR filter.

Slide 41Moving average: a simple FIR filter~9 min

The moving average sums the last N samples and divides by N. Its magnitude is the sinc-like function on the slide, with zeros at multiples of f_s over N. That is its main strength: if N T_s equals the period of a known disturbance, for example a ripple or mains frequency, the disturbance and its harmonics are removed exactly. The phase is linear with a delay of N minus one over two samples. Its weakness is the stop band: the first side lobe is only 13 dB down. It can be computed recursively with one addition and one subtraction per sample, independent of N.

Emphasise
  • Exact zeros at k f_s/N.
  • Delay (N-1)/2 samples.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why is the recursive form risky in floating point? A: Rounding errors accumulate; use integers or periodic re-summation.

Transition: The moving average is FIR; let us compare FIR and IIR in general.

Slide 42FIR versus IIR filters~10 min

The top structure is FIR: a tapped delay line with coefficients, no feedback. The bottom is IIR: the output is fed back. The table summarises the differences. FIR filters are always stable and can have exactly linear phase if the coefficients are symmetric, but need many taps for sharp selectivity and have long delay. IIR filters achieve the same selectivity with very few coefficients and low delay, but their phase is non-linear, they can become unstable, and coefficient quantisation matters, so we implement them as cascaded biquads. In feedback loops IIR filters win because delay is the enemy; FIR is used for linear phase, monitoring and offline analysis.

Emphasise
  • Loops: IIR. Linear phase: FIR.
  • IIR as biquad cascades.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Can an FIR be unstable? A: No, it has no feedback; all poles are at the origin.

Transition: Among IIR designs, two classical families matter most.

Slide 43Butterworth vs. Bessel: magnitude, group delay, step response~10 min

Both filters here are fourth order with the same minus three dB frequency of one kilohertz. Butterworth has a maximally flat magnitude and a steeper roll-off: minus 38 dB at three times the corner versus minus 25 for Bessel. But its group delay peaks near the corner, so different frequencies arrive at different times, and the step response overshoots by 10.8 percent with ringing. Bessel has maximally flat group delay, near-linear phase and only 0.8 percent overshoot. Rule: Butterworth when attenuation matters, as in anti-aliasing and noise suppression; Bessel when waveform fidelity matters, as in capturing current steps or test signals.

Emphasise
  • Butterworth: flat magnitude. Bessel: flat delay.
  • Overshoot 10.8 % vs 0.8 %.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Where are Chebyshev filters used? A: Where steepness matters more than ripple, rarely inside servo loops.

Transition: Now a filter that amplifies noise instead of removing it: the derivative.

Slide 44Filtered derivative: position to velocity to acceleration~10 min

Differentiation multiplies by omega, plus 20 dB per decade, and quantisation noise is broad-band, so derivatives amplify noise. The practical derivative s over one plus s T_f limits the high-frequency gain to one over T_f; a second filter pole makes it roll off. The formulas give the noise standard deviation of backward differences: root two times the position noise over T_s for velocity, root six over T_s squared for acceleration. With 10,000 counts per revolution at 8 kilohertz, acceleration noise is 28,000 radians per second squared: useless without filtering. The better approach is a model-based observer, as discussed on Day 3.

Emphasise
  • Noise grows by 1/T_s per derivative.
  • Use observers for acceleration.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why sqrt(6)? A: The second difference has coefficients 1, -2, 1 whose squares sum to 6.

Transition: Let us see the velocity case in the time domain.

Slide 45Velocity from a quantised encoder: raw vs. filtered~8 min

This simulation uses our 2500-line encoder at 8 kilohertz with a true speed of 60 plus minus 30 rpm. The raw difference, light blue, only takes values that are multiples of 48 rpm. The second-order low-pass at 150 hertz recovers the trend, but it lags the true speed by about 1.5 milliseconds and leaves ripple. In a velocity loop this noise is multiplied by the proportional gain and appears in the torque command, causing audible noise and heating. The root cause is resolution; filtering is a compromise between noise and phase lag.

Emphasise
  • Filtering trades noise for lag.
  • Resolution is the real fix.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What would a 23-bit encoder show? A: A nearly smooth raw estimate, with steps under 0.1 rpm.

Transition: Back to aliasing: where should the filter sit?

Slide 46Anti-aliasing: analogue before the ADC, digital after~9 min

Only an analogue filter in front of the sampler can prevent aliasing: once noise has folded into the signal band, as the red arrow shows, it is indistinguishable. The modern approach is oversampling: sample much faster than needed, use a simple RC as anti-aliasing filter, then filter sharply in the digital domain and decimate. Sigma-delta converters are the extreme case. In a control loop the analogue filter also adds delay, so keep its corner well above crossover and include it in the model. And recall that synchronous PWM sampling is itself a structural anti-aliasing measure.

Emphasise
  • Anti-aliasing must be analogue.
  • Oversample, then filter digitally and decimate.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Is the RC on the ADC input enough? A: With sufficient oversampling and a digital filter, often yes.

Transition: How do we implement these filters in firmware?

Slide 47Implementing filters: difference equations and biquads~12 min

The general IIR difference equation is shown on top. We implement it as second-order sections in direct form two transposed, which needs only two state variables per biquad and has good numerical behaviour in floating point. The C sketch is the complete per-sample code. Practical pitfalls: for very low corner frequencies relative to the sampling rate, the poles crowd near z equals one and single-precision coefficients are not accurate enough, so use double precision or error feedback. In fixed point scale carefully to avoid overflow and watch for limit cycles. Initialise the states to the steady-state value to avoid start-up transients, and reset them on mode changes.

Emphasise
  • Cascade of biquads, DF-II transposed.
  • Low f_c/f_s needs precision.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why not one high-order polynomial? A: Pole positions become extremely sensitive to coefficient rounding.

Transition: Summary of signal processing.

Slide 48Summary: signal processing and filters~5 min

Every filter has a frequency response and a delay, and in a loop both matter. Moving averages give exact zeros. FIR filters are stable with linear phase; IIR filters are cheap with low delay. Butterworth for attenuation, Bessel for waveform fidelity. Derivatives amplify noise. Aliasing must be prevented before the ADC. Walk through the filter-selection table on the right and invite questions about filters participants use in their own drives.

Emphasise
  • Pick the filter by the problem, not by habit.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Which filter would you use for current ripple? A: Usually none: synchronous sampling.

Session 4.5 · Communication and system integration + course wrap-up Curriculum code 4.5 · 45 min

Slide 50Communication architecture of a multi-axis machine~4 min

A modern machine has three communication levels. Inside the drive, currents and angle are exchanged every few tens of microseconds over SPI, sigma-delta bit streams and encoder protocols. Between motion controller and drives, set-points and actual values are exchanged every 0.125 to 4 milliseconds over a real-time fieldbus such as EtherCAT, PROFINET IRT or CANopen. Above that, HMI and plant IT use standard Ethernet. The diagram shows the line topology typical of EtherCAT and the separate encoder links.

Emphasise
  • Three levels, three time scales.

Transition: Let us start with the classic: CAN.

Slide 51CAN and CANopen~5 min

CAN, standardised in ISO 11898, is a differential two-wire bus with bitwise arbitration: the dominant zero wins, so the lowest identifier has the highest priority and no frame is lost in a collision. Classical CAN carries up to eight bytes; CAN FD up to 64. Error detection includes a 15-bit CRC and error counters. CANopen adds an object dictionary, PDOs for real-time data and SDOs for configuration. The bandwidth estimate in the box shows the limit: nine eight-byte frames per cycle at one megabit take about 1.1 milliseconds. CANopen is excellent for robust, low-cost systems with millisecond cycles and few axes.

Emphasise
  • Arbitration by identifier priority.
  • About 1 ms cycles for a few axes.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What limits CAN bit rate? A: Bit time must exceed the round-trip propagation delay on the bus; hence about 40 m at 1 Mbit/s.

Transition: The drive behaviour itself is standardised in CiA 402.

Slide 52CiA 402 drive profile~5 min

CiA 402 standardises the drive state machine, the object dictionary and the operating modes, independently of the bus. The state machine on the left goes from switch-on disabled through ready to switch on and switched on to operation enabled, driven by the controlword; the statusword reports the state. Faults lead through fault reaction to fault and need a reset. The table lists the most important objects. Modes include profile position, velocity and torque, homing, and the cyclic synchronous modes CSP, CSV and CST, where the controller interpolates and sends a new set-point every cycle. The same profile is used over CANopen and over EtherCAT as CoE.

Emphasise
  • Controlword 6040h, statusword 6041h.
  • CSP / CSV / CST for coordinated motion.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why does my drive not enable? A: Usually the controlword sequence was skipped; it must pass through each state.

Transition: EtherCAT is the dominant bus for these cyclic modes.

Slide 53EtherCAT: processing on the fly~5 min

EtherCAT uses standard Ethernet frames with EtherType 88A4, but one frame serves all slaves. Each slave has an EtherCAT slave controller chip, which reads its output data and inserts its input data while the frame passes, with a delay well below a microsecond. The last slave returns the frame, so the line is a logical ring. Each datagram carries a working counter that each addressed slave increments, so the master detects missing slaves. The example: 32 axes with 12 bytes each way fit in one datagram, about 35 microseconds on the wire plus node delays, comfortably inside a 100 microsecond cycle.

Emphasise
  • One frame, many slaves, hardware processing.
  • WKC detects missing or failed slaves.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Does EtherCAT need switches? A: No, slaves forward the frame themselves; standard switches would break the timing.

Transition: Fast cycles are only useful if all drives act at the same instant.

Slide 54Distributed clocks, synchronisation and jitter~4 min

With distributed clocks, the first DC-capable slave is the reference. The master measures propagation delays and continuously compensates offset and drift of every local clock. All drives then latch inputs and apply set-points on the same SYNC0 event, independent of when the frame arrives. Without DC, actions would follow frame arrival with its jitter, shown in red. Synchronisation is well below a microsecond. Explain the difference between latency and jitter: constant latency costs phase, variable jitter causes position error proportional to speed, for example one micrometre at one metre per second and one microsecond.

Emphasise
  • Latency costs phase, jitter costs accuracy.
  • SYNC0 decouples action from frame arrival.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Is jitter visible in contouring? A: Yes, as position error proportional to velocity and as sub-harmonic disturbances.

Slide 55Serial interfaces and fieldbus comparison~3 min

The table compares on-board SPI, RS-485, encoder protocols, CAN, EtherCAT and PROFINET IRT. SPI has no built-in error check, so add a CRC. RS-485 is the physical layer under Modbus RTU and many encoder links. Encoder protocols run every control cycle with CRC. Transition: Now we put numbers on the total latency.

Slide 56Latency budget of a distributed position loop~4 min

Follow the bar from left to right: encoder sampling and transmission, waiting for the sync event, the bus to the controller, computation, the bus back, applying the set-point at the next sync, and finally the inner loops. Altogether the dead time is two to three bus cycles plus inner-loop lag. The worked example: 250 microsecond cycle, 625 microseconds dead time. For a 30 hertz position loop that costs 6.8 degrees, acceptable. For a 200 hertz velocity loop over the bus it costs 45 degrees, not acceptable. Hence close fast loops in the drive, send position or velocity set-points, add feed-forward in the PDO, and interpolate inside the drive.

Emphasise
  • Fast loops belong in the drive.
  • Feed-forward recovers dynamics.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why not run the velocity loop in the PLC? A: The bus dead time would consume most of the phase margin.

Transition: Finally, how do we know data are valid?

Slide 57Data validity: CRC, watchdogs, timestamps~4 min

A CRC detects corrupted bits, but a perfectly valid CRC can protect an old value. So we also need freshness checks: working counters and sequence counters detect lost or frozen telegrams; watchdogs such as the EtherCAT SyncManager watchdog or CANopen heartbeat detect silence; timestamps give the age of each value. Plausibility checks add range, rate and cross-checks, for example that three phase currents sum to zero. The flow chart shows the decision: if any check fails, hold or ramp down and raise an alarm. Safety functions use separate black-channel safety protocols on the same bus.

Emphasise
  • Valid = uncorrupted AND fresh AND plausible.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Is the fieldbus CRC enough for safety? A: No, safety requires a black-channel protocol per IEC 61784-3.

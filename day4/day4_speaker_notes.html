Session 3.2 · (cont.) Motor driving: PWM, voltage and current limits Curriculum code 3.2 · 30 min (incl. agenda and recap)

Slide 2Agenda and learning objectives~2 min

Welcome to Day 4. Today we take everything from the first three days and make a motor move precisely. Read the five sessions aloud and point at the time column. Then point at the reference motor box: every number on today's plots comes from this one motor, so students can check any result with a calculator. Read the learning outcomes as promises: by the end they should be able to explain, sketch and calculate these things.

Emphasise
  • One motor, one set of numbers, all day.
  • Common misconception: Students may think they must memorise the motor data; tell them it is printed on every relevant slide.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why one motor all day? A: So results connect: the current-loop speed from 3.4 limits the velocity loop in 3.5.

Transition: First, a quick look back at what we already know.

Slide 3What we learned so far (1/2): motors and feedback~3 min

Recap Day 1: current makes torque, T equals k_t times i; spinning makes back-EMF; each winding is a resistor plus an inductor; and Newton's law for rotation links torque to acceleration. Recap Day 2: feedback means measure, compare, correct, exactly like cruise control in a car. PID reacts to the present error, the accumulated past error and the trend. A Bode plot shows gain and phase against frequency, and phase margin is our safety distance from oscillation. Ask two students to restate one item each.

Emphasise
  • T = k_t i and J dω/dt = T − T_L will be used constantly.
  • Common misconception: Some think feedback needs a perfect model; it does not, it corrects errors it measures.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What does the I in PID do? A: It removes steady error by adding up the error over time.

Transition: Now Day 3 and the plan for today.

Slide 4What we learned so far (2/2): the inverter, and today's chain~3 min

Day 3 gave us the inverter: transistors are electronic switches; two in series form a leg whose output is either the plus rail or zero; three legs feed the three motor phases from one DC voltage, the DC link. Show the photo of a real IGBT module. Then walk down the chain on the right: switching, then voltage to current with FOC, then current to torque to speed to position, then tuning, then disturbances. Each box is one session of today.

Emphasise
  • Today = one chain from switch commands to motion.
  • Common misconception: Students sometimes think the inverter makes sine waves directly; it only switches, the sine is an average.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Can a leg output half the DC voltage? A: Not instantaneously, only on average, which is the next topic.

Transition: So how do on/off switches create a smooth voltage? Duty cycle.

Slide 6Refresher: duty cycle and average voltage~3 min

Use the light-switch analogy: flick a switch on and off a thousand times a second and the room looks dimmed. The duty cycle D is the fraction of the period the switch is on. The average voltage is simply D times the DC voltage. Work the example aloud: 25 of 62.5 microseconds is 0.4, times 325 volts gives 130 volts. The motor inductance smooths the current like a flywheel smooths a pedalling rhythm. Changing D slowly gives a slowly changing average, and that is how we will make a sine.

Emphasise
  • Average voltage = D · V_dc.
  • Inductance smooths the current.
  • Common misconception: Many think the motor sees 130 V continuously; it actually sees 325 V pulses and averages them.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What duty gives 0 V average? A: D = 0 for a single leg; between legs we compare duties.

Transition: Now let us vary the duty sinusoidally.

Slide 7The three-phase inverter and sinusoidal PWM~3 min

Each leg compares its sine reference with a fast triangle. Whenever the sine is above the triangle, the upper switch is on. Point at the middle plot: the pulses get wider when the sine is high. The bottom plot shows the line-to-line voltage, a pulse train whose average follows the orange sine. The modulation index m is the sine amplitude relative to the triangle; the peak phase voltage is m times V_dc over two. Example: m = 0.8 gives 130 volts.

Emphasise
  • The duty cycle follows the sine, so the average voltage is a sine.
  • Common misconception: Students confuse the switching frequency with the motor frequency; there are 15 or more pulses per motor period.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What happens if m > 1? A: The sine exceeds the triangle, pulses saturate and the waveform distorts.

Transition: Sine PWM wastes some DC voltage. Let us see how to get more.

Slide 8Getting more voltage: space vectors and SVPWM~3 min

Three legs, each up or down, give eight switch states. Each state produces one voltage arrow; six of them point to the corners of a hexagon and two are zero. To create any arrow inside the hexagon, the drive alternates between the two neighbouring arrows within one PWM period, like mixing two paints in different amounts. The biggest circle fitting inside the hexagon has radius V_dc over root three, fifteen percent more than sine PWM. Mention the dwell-time formulas only as going further.

Emphasise
  • SVPWM gives 15 % more voltage than sine PWM.
  • Common misconception: It is not a new kind of hardware, only a smarter switching pattern.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why not use the corners of the hexagon? A: Then the arrow length would vary while rotating, distorting the current.

Transition: Real switches are not ideal; first problem: dead time.

Slide 9Dead time: why it is needed and what it costs~3 min

If both switches of a leg are on at once, the DC link is short-circuited: shoot-through. So the drive waits a tiny dead time before turning a switch on. During that time a diode carries the current, and the output voltage depends on the current direction, not on what we commanded. The error is t_d times f_sw times V_dc: 5.2 volts in the example, which is as big as the whole back-EMF at 100 rpm. This is why slow motion is hardest.

Emphasise
  • Dead time prevents shoot-through but creates a voltage error.
  • Common misconception: Students think the error is negligible because 1 µs is tiny; it repeats 16,000 times per second.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Does a higher switching frequency help? A: No, it makes the dead-time error larger.

Transition: Speaking of switching frequency: how is it chosen?

Slide 10Switching frequency: a trade-off~3 min

Faster switching gives smaller current ripple and shorter control delay, but each switching event costs a bit of energy as heat, and creates electrical noise. Below about 16 kHz the motor whines audibly. Work the ripple example: 0.083 times 325 divided by 3 mH times 16 kHz gives about 0.56 A. Typical servo drives use 8 to 16 kHz. Use the light-switch analogy again: flicking faster gives smoother light but tires your hand.

Emphasise
  • Ripple goes down with f_sw, losses go up.
  • Common misconception: There is no single best frequency; it is a trade-off.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why do SiC transistors allow higher f_sw? A: They lose less energy per switching event.

Transition: Finally, the limits that bound what the motor can do.

Slide 11Voltage and current limits: the torque–speed envelope~3 min

Two limits shape the plot. The current limit protects transistors and windings from heat; with k_t = 0.72 and 20 A it gives 14.4 Nm maximum torque. The voltage limit: the back-EMF grows with speed, but the inverter can give at most V_dc over root three, 188 volts. Dividing by ψ_PM gives 1563 rad/s electrical, about 3730 rpm without load; resistive and inductive drops lower this to about 3240 rpm at full torque. Above base speed, torque falls; field weakening is going-further material.

Emphasise
  • Current limit → max torque; voltage limit → max speed.
  • Common misconception: Students often think more current can always give more speed; above base speed the voltage, not the current, is the problem.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: How do you raise base speed? A: Higher DC voltage or a motor with smaller ψ_PM.

Transition: Let us check understanding.

Slide 12Check your understanding – 3.2~2 min

Give students two minutes in pairs. Answers: (1) 0.25 × 400 V = 100 V. (2) Both on at once short-circuits the DC link (shoot-through); the fix, dead time, costs a current-direction-dependent voltage error. (3) The voltage limit: the back-EMF has nearly reached the maximum inverter voltage, so no voltage is left to push more current in.

Emphasise
  • Use the answers to spot who still confuses average and instantaneous voltage.
  • Common misconception: Question 3 often gets answered with 'current limit'; insist on the distinction.

Transition: Now the summary of the session.

Slide 13Summary 3.2 – PWM and limits~2 min

Read the five take-aways and the key terms. Ask one student to define duty cycle and another to define base speed in their own words. This closes the PWM part; after this we move from voltages to the clever coordinate trick that makes AC motors controllable like DC motors.

Emphasise
  • Duty cycle, SVPWM, dead time, base speed are the four terms to keep.

Transition: Next session: field-oriented control.

Session 3.3 · Field-oriented control (FOC) Curriculum code 3.3 · 90 min

Slide 15Why field-oriented control? The DC-motor analogy~6 min

In a brushed DC motor, the commutator automatically keeps the current at ninety degrees to the magnet, so torque is simply k_t times current. In a PM synchronous motor the magnet is on the rotor and turns, so the current direction must turn with it. Use the door analogy: you push hardest when you push perpendicular to the door, even while it swings. FOC measures the rotor angle and splits the current into i_d along the magnet, which makes no torque, and i_q at ninety degrees, which makes torque.

Emphasise
  • FOC is an electronic commutator.
  • i_q makes torque, i_d does not (for our motor).
  • Common misconception: Students think FOC changes the motor; it only changes how we compute the currents.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What happens without the angle measurement? A: We cannot know where to push, so torque per amp drops or reverses.

Transition: To understand FOC we need two refreshers: three-phase sines and rotations.

Slide 16Refresher: three-phase sinusoids, 120° apart~7 min

Draw attention to the red line at zero degrees: i_a is 1, i_b and i_c are minus one half, the sum is zero. That is true at every instant, so only two currents are independent. Now the key physical picture: three coils placed 120 degrees apart around the stator, fed with currents 120 degrees apart in time, together produce one magnetic field arrow of constant length that rotates. If you have time, sketch it on the board at 0, 90 and 180 degrees.

Emphasise
  • Three wiggling numbers = one rotating arrow.
  • Sum of the three currents is zero.
  • Common misconception: Students expect three separate fields; they add to one.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why 120 degrees? A: Three phases share one full turn of 360 degrees equally.

Transition: How do we describe a rotating arrow mathematically? Rotation matrices.

Slide 17Refresher: rotating vectors and rotation matrices~7 min

A 2-D vector can be written as a complex number; multiplying by e to the j theta rotates it. The rotation matrix does the same with cos and sin. Do the example: rotating (1,0) by 90 degrees gives (0,1). Then the key twist: if you rotate the axes instead of the vector, the vector appears rotated the other way. The point (3,4) seen from axes turned by 90 degrees reads (4, minus 3). That is exactly what Park does: it rotates the observer.

Emphasise
  • Length is preserved, only direction changes.
  • Rotating the axes by +θ = rotating the vector by −θ.
  • Common misconception: Sign confusion is common; check with the 90-degree example.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What is R(θ) times R(−θ)? A: The identity: rotate and rotate back.

Transition: First transform: from three phases to two axes.

Slide 18Clarke transform: three phases → two axes (αβ)~6 min

Each phase current pushes along its own coil axis. Add the three pushes as arrows, head to tail as in the drawing, and write the result in x-y form: that is Clarke. With the sum equal to zero, i_alpha is just i_a and i_beta needs only i_a and i_b, so two sensors suffice. Work the example: 8, minus 4, minus 4 amps gives an arrow of 8 amps along alpha. The two-thirds factor makes the arrow length equal the peak phase current.

Emphasise
  • Clarke = three numbers to two, nothing lost.
  • Arrow still rotates: i_α, i_β are still sines.
  • Common misconception: Students think Clarke removes the AC nature; it does not, that is Park's job.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why only two current sensors? A: The third current follows from the zero sum.

Transition: Now we ride along with the rotor.

Slide 19Park transform: looking at the rotor from a rotating camera~8 min

This is the central idea of the day. Imagine a camera bolted to the rotor, filming it. In that film the magnet stands still. The current arrow rotates at exactly the same speed, so in the film it also stands still: two constant numbers, i_d and i_q. Mathematically we rotate the axes by theta_e, which is the same as rotating the vector by minus theta_e, as in the refresher. Same physical arrow, new coordinates: AC becomes DC.

Emphasise
  • Park needs the rotor angle θ_e.
  • In the rotor frame, steady currents are constants.
  • Common misconception: Students think the currents physically become DC; only the description changes, the motor still carries sines.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What if the camera rotates at the wrong speed? A: The arrow would slowly drift in the picture: i_d and i_q oscillate.

Transition: Let us see this in a plot.

Slide 20The same current seen in the three frames~5 min

Top: three sines, with a step from 5 to 10 amps halfway. Middle: two sines after Clarke. Bottom: after Park, two flat lines; i_d is zero and i_q makes a clean step. Remind students of Day 2: a PI controller drives a constant error to zero but struggles with sines. In dq we only have constants, so a simple PI works at any speed.

Emphasise
  • In dq, a PI controller sees constants.
  • Common misconception: Students may think the step should look like a sine envelope in dq; it is just a step.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Where would a load change show up? A: As a change in i_q.

Transition: One more detail about angles: electrical versus mechanical.

Slide 21Electrical vs. mechanical angle: θ_e = p θ_m~5 min

Our motor has four pole pairs, eight magnet poles. Every time the rotor passes one pole pair, the current must go through one full electrical cycle, so one mechanical turn equals four electrical turns. Show the saw-tooth plot. Example: 3000 rpm gives 200 Hz electrical. Practical consequence: an angle error of 2 mechanical degrees becomes 8 electrical degrees.

Emphasise
  • θ_e = p θ_m; f_e = p n / 60.
  • Common misconception: Students forget p and get frequencies four times too small.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: 8-pole motor at 1500 rpm? A: p = 4, f_e = 4 · 1500 / 60 = 100 Hz.

Transition: With the frame in place, what does the motor look like from the rotor?

Slide 22The motor seen by the rotating camera: two R–L circuits~8 min

Viewed from the rotor, each axis is the winding from Day 1: a resistor and an inductor, time constant L over R, 6 ms. Two extra voltage sources appear because the frame rotates: the coupling terms omega L i, which link the axes, and the back-EMF, which appears only on the q axis. Point at the equation box and match each term to the circuit. No need to derive it; the derivation is going further.

Emphasise
  • Each axis = R–L circuit plus speed-dependent sources.
  • Back-EMF only on q.
  • Common misconception: Students think the coupling terms are a new physical effect; they come from the rotating viewpoint.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: When do the coupling terms vanish? A: At standstill, ω_e = 0.

Transition: Now the most useful equation: torque.

Slide 23Torque comes from i_q~7 min

For our surface-magnet motor, torque is three-halves p psi_PM times i_q, which is k_t times i_q. Compute k_t = 1.5 · 4 · 0.12 = 0.72 Nm/A; 10 A gives 7.2 Nm. If the current arrow is tilted by beta from the q axis, only cos beta of it makes torque, as the blue curve shows. The dashed and orange curves are for interior-magnet motors, where extra reluctance torque appears: MTPA and field weakening are going further.

Emphasise
  • T = k_t i_q; keep i_d = 0 for our motor.
  • Common misconception: Students may think negative i_d always helps; only in IPM motors or above base speed.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why is i_d = 0 best here? A: It gives all the current to torque, minimum copper loss per Nm.

Transition: Let us put numbers together in a worked example.

Slide 24Worked example: operating point at 3000 rpm~8 min

Go through the four steps slowly with units. Step 1: omega_e = 4 · 314.2 = 1257 rad/s. Step 2: back-EMF 150.8 V. Step 3: v_q = 5 + 150.8 = 155.8 V, v_d = −37.7 V. Step 4: length 160.3 V, below the 188 V limit, so 85 % is used. Then the vector diagram: the voltage arrow is mostly back-EMF, tilted by the omega L i term. At higher load or speed the arrow grows and hits the limit.

Emphasise
  • Most of the voltage at speed is back-EMF.
  • Common misconception: Students add v_d and v_q arithmetically; they must use Pythagoras.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What if i_q doubled to 20 A? A: v_d doubles to −75 V and the voltage length rises to about 175 V, still just under the limit.

Transition: Now the complete FOC block diagram.

Slide 25FOC: the basic structure~7 min

Follow the numbered steps under the diagram, tracing with a pointer. Every PWM period, 62.5 microseconds: measure two phase currents and the angle, transform with Clarke and Park, run two PI controllers, transform back with inverse Park, compute new duty cycles with SVPWM. The velocity controller sits outside and provides i_q*. The orange decoupling block adds the known coupling terms; that is going further.

Emphasise
  • The whole loop runs 16,000 times per second.
  • Common misconception: Students think FOC is a single block; it is a chain of simple steps.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Which signal sets the torque? A: i_q*, from the velocity controller.

Transition: The chain only works if the angle is right.

Slide 26Rotor angle alignment and offset errors~6 min

The encoder's zero is wherever it happened to be mounted. FOC needs angle zero exactly at the magnet's d axis. A wrong offset is like a crooked camera: we push at the wrong angle. Torque falls with cos of the error and wasted current grows with sin. Example: 2 degrees mechanical becomes 8 electrical: 1 % torque loss but 14 % wasted current. Beyond 90 degrees torque reverses and the motor runs away.

Emphasise
  • Offset errors waste current and can cause runaway.
  • Common misconception: Students think a small offset only costs a little torque; it also costs heat.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: How is the offset found? A: Factory-stored for absolute encoders, or by DC alignment.

Transition: Time to check understanding.

Slide 27Check your understanding – 3.3~5 min

Pairs for two minutes. Answers: (1) i_alpha = 8 A, i_beta = 0 A. (2) The camera rotates with the rotor at the same speed as the current arrow, so the arrow appears stationary: constant i_d and i_q. (3) 8 poles means p = 4: f_e = 4 · 1500 / 60 = 100 Hz.

Emphasise
  • Question 2 tests the core intuition of the session.
  • Common misconception: Watch for answers that say 'the currents become DC in the wires'.

Transition: Summary of FOC.

Slide 28Summary 3.3 – Field-oriented control~5 min

Read the take-aways. Emphasise the story: three currents are one rotating arrow, Clarke writes it in two coordinates, Park views it from the rotor so it becomes constant, torque is k_t i_q, and FOC runs this chain every PWM period. Go through the key terms; MTPA and field weakening are labelled as going further.

Emphasise
  • The rotating-camera picture is the one idea to remember.

Transition: Break, then the control loops around FOC.

Session 3.4 · Current, velocity and position loops Curriculum code 3.4 · 90 min

Slide 30The nested (cascade) control structure~8 min

Use the analogy: a navigator decides where to be (position), tells the cruise control how fast to go (velocity), which tells the engine management how much torque to make (current). Each loop treats the closed loop inside it as its actuator. Inner loops are faster and enforce limits. The dashed paths are feedforward: we send known commands straight to the inner loops. Point at the sampling rates: 16, 8 and 2 kHz.

Emphasise
  • Inner loops faster; each loop sees the inner loop as its actuator.
  • Common misconception: Students may think all three loops control the same variable.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why not one big controller? A: Cascades are easier to tune, and inner loops can enforce limits.

Transition: We design from the inside, starting with the current loop.

Slide 31Current-loop PI: a step-by-step recipe~10 min

The plant is the R–L winding plus a small delay from computing and PWM. The PI's zero is placed to cancel the slow winding pole: T_i = L/R. Then K_p is set so the delay is still safe: K_p = L/(2 T_sigma). Go through the four steps: T_sigma = 93.75 µs, T_i = 6 ms, K_p = 16 V/A, crossover about 850 Hz, and the delay eats 29 degrees, leaving 61 degrees phase margin. This rule is called the technical optimum.

Emphasise
  • T_i = L/R; K_p = L/(2T_σ).
  • The delay sets the speed limit.
  • Common misconception: Students think a bigger K_p is always better; the delay makes it oscillate.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Doubling f_s? A: Halves T_σ, doubles K_p and the bandwidth.

Transition: Let us check it on a Bode plot and step.

Slide 32Current loop: computed frequency and step response~8 min

Left: Bode plot of the loop. Without delay, the phase stays at minus 90. With delay, the phase bends down at high frequency; at the crossover around 850 Hz we still have 61 degrees margin. Right: the step response. The current reaches 10 A in about 0.3 ms with only 3.9 % overshoot, close to the prediction. Connect to Day 2: phase margin is the safety distance.

Emphasise
  • Delay bends the phase down; it limits K_p.
  • Common misconception: Students may read the closed-loop curve as the open-loop one; point at the legend.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Where would instability start? A: When the phase reaches −180° at gain 1.

Transition: Now the velocity loop.

Slide 33Velocity-loop PI: a step-by-step recipe~10 min

From current to speed is Newton's law: torque accelerates the inertia, so speed is the integral, an integrator. There is also a small lag from the closed current loop and speed filter. Choose a spacing factor a between 2 and 4. Steps: T_eq = 0.5 ms; with a = 2, crossover = 1000 rad/s; K_p = J omega_c / k_t = 1.67; T_i = 2 ms. Larger a is slower but calmer. The symmetric optimum formulas are going further.

Emphasise
  • K_p ∝ J: heavier load needs more gain.
  • Common misconception: Students think velocity and current loops are tuned the same way; the plant is different (integrator).
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why does K_p scale with J? A: To give the same acceleration per rad/s of error.

Transition: See the result.

Slide 34Velocity loop: computed Bode plot and step responses~8 min

Compare a = 2 and a = 3. a = 2 gives a faster loop, 164 Hz, but only 35 degrees margin and 46 % overshoot. a = 3: 107 Hz, 53 degrees and 25 % overshoot. The dashed curves add a reference prefilter that smooths the command and almost removes the overshoot. Key lesson: more phase margin means less overshoot.

Emphasise
  • Phase margin ↔ overshoot.
  • Common misconception: Students may think the prefilter changes stability; it only shapes the command.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Which would you pick for a robot? A: Usually a = 3 or more, for robustness.

Transition: Now the outermost loop: position.

Slide 35Position loop: a P controller and the following error~8 min

With a good velocity loop, position is just the integral of speed, so a simple P controller already reaches a fixed target with zero error. But when following a moving target at constant speed, a constant lag remains: v over k_v. Dog-on-a-leash analogy. Steps: 10 m/min is 167 mm/s, divided by 50 per second gives 3.3 mm. That is huge for machining; feedforward will fix it.

Emphasise
  • e = v / k_v.
  • No overshoot allowed in machining.
  • Common misconception: Students think zero error at rest means zero error while moving.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Two axes with different k_v cutting a circle? A: The circle becomes an ellipse.

Transition: What happens when commands hit limits?

Slide 36Torque and speed limits; integrator windup~8 min

When the current hits its limit, the loop is effectively open, but the integrator keeps adding up error. When the limit releases, the stored error causes huge overshoot: 553 instead of 300 rad/s in the plot. Analogy: pressing the accelerator to the floor and still mentally 'pushing harder'. Anti-windup stops integrating while saturated, giving the blue curve.

Emphasise
  • Every PI with a limit needs anti-windup.
  • Common misconception: Students think limits only clip; they also corrupt the integrator.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Does the current loop need anti-windup? A: Yes, against the voltage limit.

Transition: Smooth commands help too: trajectories.

Slide 37Trajectory generation: trapezoidal vs. S-curve profiles~8 min

A trapezoidal profile ramps speed, so acceleration jumps instantly, which jolts the machine. An S-curve also ramps the acceleration, limiting jerk. It takes 6 % longer but is much smoother. Lift analogy: a good lift starts so gently you hardly notice. The peak torque J alpha = 3.6 Nm, 5 A, is within limits.

Emphasise
  • Jerk = rate of change of acceleration; limit it.
  • Common misconception: Students think the fastest profile is always best; vibrations cost more time.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why output acceleration too? A: For torque feedforward.

Transition: Now feedforward against following error.

Slide 38Following error and how feedforward removes it~9 min

Log scale. With P only, the error peaks at 3000 mrad. Adding velocity feedforward, sending the planned speed straight to the velocity loop, reduces it to 2.6 mrad. Adding torque feedforward, sending the needed current J alpha / k_t, reduces it to 0.19. Feedforward predicts; feedback only fixes what is left. Because it acts outside the loop, it does not change stability.

Emphasise
  • Feedforward reduces error by orders of magnitude without affecting stability.
  • Common misconception: Students assume more feedback gain is the only way to reduce error.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What if Ĵ is wrong by 10 %? A: The leftover error is roughly 10 % of the acceleration part; feedback corrects it.

Transition: Check understanding.

Slide 39Check your understanding – 3.4~7 min

Answers: (1) K_p = L/(2T_σ) = 0.006 / 0.0002 = 30 V/A. (2) e = 0.2 / 40 = 0.005 m = 5 mm. (3) Each outer loop is designed assuming the inner loop is already closed and fast; tuning the outer loop first means designing against the wrong plant.

Emphasise
  • Units at every step.
  • Common misconception: In (2) students forget to convert to mm.

Transition: Summary.

Slide 40Summary 3.4 – Cascade loops~6 min

Read the take-aways: cascade with inner loops faster; two recipes; following error; anti-windup, S-curves and feedforward. Ask students to state the current-loop recipe from memory. Go through the key terms.

Emphasise
  • The two PI recipes.

Transition: Break, then tuning and bandwidth.

Session 3.5 · Loop tuning and bandwidth Curriculum code 3.5 · 60 min

Slide 42Refresher: what does “bandwidth” mean?~7 min

Feed a loop sine commands of increasing frequency. Slow sines are followed perfectly; fast ones come out smaller and late. The bandwidth is where the output drops to 70 %, minus 3 dB. Dancer analogy. In time, higher bandwidth means faster response: rise time is about 0.35 over bandwidth. 100 Hz gives 3.5 ms, 400 Hz gives 0.9 ms. Compare the two curves in both plots.

Emphasise
  • Bandwidth = −3 dB frequency; t_r ≈ 0.35/f_BW.
  • Common misconception: Students confuse bandwidth with switching frequency or crossover; crossover is the open-loop gain-1 point, bandwidth is closed-loop, roughly similar.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Is higher bandwidth always better? A: Faster, but more sensitive to noise and resonances.

Transition: How bandwidths stack up in a drive.

Slide 43The bandwidth hierarchy of a servo drive~6 min

On a log axis: PWM at 8–32 kHz, current loop 0.5–3 kHz, velocity 50–400 Hz, position 10–80 Hz. Each loop is a few times slower than the one inside it. The current loop is limited by delays, velocity by mechanical resonance, position by velocity. Robots with elastic gears reach only a few tens of hertz.

Emphasise
  • Each loop slower than its inner one.
  • Common misconception: Students think the drive alone sets bandwidth; mechanics usually dominate.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why is a robot slower than a direct drive? A: Elastic gears create low resonances.

Transition: How do we tune in practice?

Slide 44Tuning procedure: from the inside out~6 min

Walk down the flowchart. Emphasise measuring instead of guessing; work the inertia example: 0.72 times 5 divided by 3000 equals 1.2·10^-3 kg m². Then raise gains until a margin limit is hit and back off. Check large steps last.

Emphasise
  • Inside out; measure J.
  • Common misconception: Students tune all loops at once and get confused.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why re-check after adding a notch? A: Filters cost phase.

Transition: How far apart must the loops be?

Slide 45Bandwidth separation: rule of thumb 3–10×~6 min

A velocity loop designed assuming a perfect current loop. If the current loop is only 1.5 times faster, the red curve rings; at 10 times it is clean. The phase lost is arctan of the ratio: 34 degrees at 1.5, 18 at 3, 6 at 10.

Emphasise
  • 3–10× separation.
  • Common misconception: Students think 'just make the inner loop infinitely fast'; delays prevent that.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What is lost at 20×? A: Nothing in stability, but performance is left unused.

Transition: The load inertia also changes the loop.

Slide 46Effect of the load inertia ratio~6 min

Same gains, different loads. More inertia: lower crossover, sluggish. Less inertia: higher crossover, close to the delays, little phase margin, ringing. Bicycle-gear analogy: the gear or screw ratio changes how heavy the load feels to the motor. Retune with measured J; keep the ratio below 3 to 10.

Emphasise
  • Loop gain ∝ 1/J.
  • Common misconception: Students think removing load always makes control easier.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Inertia doubles, gains fixed? A: Crossover roughly halves.

Transition: Delays: the hidden cost.

Slide 47Delays and their phase cost at crossover~6 min

A pure delay costs phase linearly with frequency: 360 times f_c times T_d. Shower analogy: a long pipe makes you overshoot the temperature. Work the example: 0.5 ms at 160 Hz costs 28.8 degrees. Go through the delay table.

Emphasise
  • Δφ = 360° f_c T_d.
  • Common misconception: Students expect delay to reduce gain; it only shifts phase.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: 200 µs at 250 Hz? A: 18°.

Transition: How do we measure all this on a real machine?

Slide 48Measuring frequency responses (FRFs)~6 min

Add a small test signal inside the running loop and compare input and output amplitude and phase at each frequency; the result is a measured Bode plot. The machine stays under control. Amplitude: above friction and noise, below limits. Modern drives do this automatically.

Emphasise
  • Measure in closed loop.
  • Common misconception: Students think you must open the loop to measure the plant.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why a chirp? A: It covers many frequencies quickly.

Transition: What do we typically find? Resonance.

Slide 49A measured-style FRF: resonance and notch filter~7 min

A springy shaft between motor and load gives a dip at 300 Hz and a peak at 600 Hz. Swing analogy: pushed at its own frequency it swings hugely. The peak can lift the loop gain above 1 where the phase is bad: buzzing. A notch filter cuts a narrow band around the peak. More on Day 5.

Emphasise
  • Resonance can destabilise the velocity loop; notch it.
  • Common misconception: Students think a notch fixes everything; it costs phase below the notch.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What if the load changes? A: The resonance moves; the notch must follow.

Transition: Check understanding.

Slide 50Check your understanding – 3.5~6 min

Answers: (1) t_r ≈ 0.35/200 Hz = 1.75 ms. (2) 360 · 250 · 0.0002 = 18°. (3) Loop gain ∝ k_t/J, so the crossover roughly halves and the response becomes slower.

Emphasise
  • Link each answer to its formula.
  • Common misconception: For (3) some say 'unstable'; it actually becomes sluggish.

Session 3.6 · Disturbances and compensation + day wrap-up Curriculum code 3.6 · 30 min (incl. wrap-up)

Slide 53Disturbances: load torque and friction~4 min

Newton's law with two disturbances: load torque, like cutting forces or gravity, and friction. Friction is harder to overcome at start than while sliding, giving stick-slip. The PI removes a constant load in the end but a dip happens first.

Emphasise
  • Disturbances enter at the motor's torque.
  • Common misconception: Friction is not just a constant; it jumps at zero speed.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why is a vertical axis special? A: Gravity is a permanent load torque.

Transition: Known effects: feedforward.

Slide 54Feedforward: velocity, acceleration/torque and friction~4 min

Feedforward predicts the needed current from the plan: J alpha plus friction, divided by k_t. Example: (3.6 + 0.1) / 0.72 = 5.1 A. Feedback only corrects what is left; stability is unchanged.

Emphasise
  • Predict, then correct.
  • Common misconception: Feedforward cannot handle unknown disturbances.

Transition: Unknown disturbances: the observer.

Slide 55Disturbance observer (DOB): estimate the unknown push~5 min

Compare what the motor should do, k_t i_q, with what it actually does, J times acceleration. The difference is the unknown push. Add it back to the current command and it cancels. Result: dip 1.85 to 0.70 rad/s. Bonus: the estimate detects collisions. The filter design is going further.

Emphasise
  • DOB = should vs. does.
  • Common misconception: Students think the DOB needs a load sensor; it uses only current and speed.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why the low-pass filter? A: Differentiating speed amplifies noise.

Transition: Periodic disturbances: cogging.

Slide 56Cogging and torque ripple compensation~3 min

Turn an unpowered motor by hand and feel the clicks: cogging. It repeats every 360 over LCM(slots, poles): 15 degrees for 12 slots, 8 poles. Measure the needed i_q versus angle at slow speed, store it in a table, add it.

Emphasise
  • Periodic in angle → look-up table.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: 9 slots/6 poles? A: LCM 18 → 20°.

Transition: Check understanding.

Slide 57Check your understanding – 3.6~4 min

Answers: (1) Constant torque feedforward equal to the gravity torque, plus integral action or a DOB for the remainder. (2) It compares the torque the motor should produce, k_t i_q, with what the measured acceleration shows, J dω/dt; the difference is the disturbance. (3) LCM(9,6) = 18, period 20°.

Emphasise
  • Known → feedforward; unknown → observer.

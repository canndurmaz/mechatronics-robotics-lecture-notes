Session 1.1 · Fundamental principles and classification Curriculum code 1.1 · 30 min · slides 2–10

Slide 2Agenda and learning objectives~3 min

Welcome. Today we learn how an electric motor makes torque, how to describe it with two simple equations, how gears and screws change what the motor feels, how to check a motor is big enough, and how springs, masses and circuits share one kind of equation. Read the objectives aloud: they are verbs you will be able to do tonight. Nothing today needs more than calculus, Newton's laws and basic circuits; where something new appears we do a short refresher first.

Emphasise
  • Five hours net; breaks are extra.
  • Misconception: 'motors are black boxes' – today we open them.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Do we need machine theory beforehand? A: No. We start from the force on a wire in a magnetic field.

Transition: Let us start with the big picture.

Slide 4A servo axis is a chain of energy converters~5 min

Follow the orange arrows: power flows from the mains through the drive, the motor, the transmission into the load. The controller compares the encoder reading with the desired position and tells the drive what to do. The refresher box recalls P = T·ω: like P = F·v for a car, but rotating. Work the example: 3000 rpm is 314 rad/s, times 1.27 N·m is about 400 W. Every block loses some power as heat, and heat is what limits a motor.

Emphasise
  • Always convert rpm to rad/s before using P = Tω.
  • Misconception: power is 'torque times rpm' – units must be rad/s.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Where does braking energy go? A: Back into the drive's capacitors, then usually into a brake resistor as heat.

Transition: What physics actually makes the torque?

Slide 5Refresher: magnetic field and the force on a wire~5 min

Picture the field as arrows from N to S. Put a wire across them and send current through: the wire is pushed sideways. Students may have seen this as the 'jumping wire' experiment. The force is F = B·l·i. Show the right-hand rule with your hand. Do the example together: 0.5 T, 10 cm, 4 A gives 0.2 N – about the weight of 20 grams. Small for one wire, which is why motors use many turns.

Emphasise
  • Force is perpendicular to both current and field.
  • Misconception: the magnet 'attracts' the wire – it pushes it sideways.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What if I reverse the current? A: The force reverses direction.

Transition: Now add motion and we get the second half of the story.

Slide 6Lorentz force and Faraday's law~5 min

Two effects always happen together. Current in a field gives force – motor action. A wire moving through a field gets a voltage – generator action, the back-EMF. For a coil both depend on the same geometry, so the torque constant equals the back-EMF constant in SI units. Walk through the three numbered steps: k = 0.04, 0.2 N·m at 5 A, 12 V at 300 rad/s. Stress the slogan: current makes torque, speed makes voltage.

Emphasise
  • k_t = k_e in SI units.
  • Misconception: back-EMF is a fault – it is unavoidable and useful.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why are k_t and k_e different on datasheets? A: Different units (V per 1000 rpm, rms values); after conversion they match.

Transition: There is a second way to make force – without magnets on the rotor.

Slide 7Reluctance force~3 min

Use the fridge-magnet analogy: steel moves to where the magnetic path is easiest. A plain iron bar near a coil turns to line up with it. Torque goes with current squared, so current direction does not matter. Point at the plot: torque is zero where the inductance is at its maximum or minimum. Mention steppers use this. The co-energy method is in small print as 'going further' – do not derive it.

Emphasise
  • Torque ∝ i², towards highest inductance.
  • Misconception: you always need two magnets for force.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why does current direction not matter? A: Because the iron is magnetised by the coil itself; flipping the current flips both.

Transition: Let us sort the many kinds of actuators.

Slide 8Classification of motors and actuators~3 min

Walk the tree left to right: brushed DC, brushless magnet motors, induction, stepper and reluctance, linear. On the right, non-electric options: hydraulics, pneumatics, piezo. The table explains why the magnet servo motor dominates factory automation: high force per size, precise, clean and easy to control. Hydraulics wins on raw force, piezo on nanometre precision with tiny strokes.

Emphasise
  • PM servo motor is the default for precise motion.
  • Misconception: pneumatics is good for precise positioning – air is springy.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Where are hydraulics still used? A: Presses, excavators, very large forces.

Transition: Time to check what stuck.

Slide 9Session 1.1 – Check your understanding~4 min

Give two minutes alone, then pairs. Answers: (1) Torque doubles, from 0.2 to 0.4 N·m, because T = k_t·i is linear. (2) 1500 rpm is 157 rad/s; P = 2 × 157 ≈ 314 W. (3) Back-EMF e = k_e·ω grows with speed; the supply must exceed it to push current in, otherwise no current, no torque.

Emphasise
  • Ask students to write the formula before the numbers.
  • Misconception in Q2: multiplying by rpm gives 3000 'W' – wrong units.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why can't speed grow forever? A: Back-EMF eventually equals the supply voltage.

Transition: Let us summarise.

Slide 10Session 1.1 – Summary and key terms~2 min

Four messages: motors convert electrical to mechanical power and back; current in a field gives force, motion gives voltage; iron alone is pulled into a field; current limits torque, voltage limits speed, heat limits continuous use. Read the key terms aloud; students should be able to explain each in one sentence.

Emphasise
  • k_t = k_e, P = Tω.
  • Three limits: current, voltage, heat.

Transition: Now we open up real machines.

Session 1.2 · Structure of electrical machines Curriculum code 1.2 · 60 min · slides 11–18

Slide 12Anatomy of an industrial servo motor~7 min

Go through the cut drawing: housing, stator stack with copper windings, magnet rotor on the shaft, bearings, brake, encoder. The air gap is thinner than a millimetre and all torque is made there. The stator gets hot, so it sits against the housing where heat escapes. The rotor is long and slim so it has little inertia. If you have a real motor or a photo, pass it around.

Emphasise
  • Stator fixed, rotor turns, torque made in the air gap.
  • Misconception: the magnets are in the housing – in a servo motor they are on the rotor.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why is there a brake? A: To hold vertical axes when power is off.

Transition: Start with the classic: the brushed DC motor.

Slide 13Brushed DC motor: construction and the commutator~9 min

Magnets sit in the stator, coils on the rotor. Problem: as the rotor turns, a coil passing under the other pole would push backwards. The commutator is a rotating switch that flips each coil's current at the right moment so torque always pushes the same way. The small plot shows torque with one coil (big dips) and three coils (much smoother). Downsides: brushes wear, spark and the heat is trapped in the rotor.

Emphasise
  • Commutator = mechanical switch synchronised with the rotor.
  • Misconception: DC current in the coils stays DC – it is switched back and forth.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why do cheap toys still use brushed motors? A: Simplest drive: just connect a battery.

Transition: What if we replace the mechanical switch by electronics?

Slide 14Brushless motors and electronic commutation~12 min

Turn the DC motor inside out: magnets on the rotor, coils on the stator. Now nothing rotating needs current, so no brushes, and heat is generated where it can escape. But someone must switch the coils at the right moment: the drive, using a sensor that tells it the rotor angle. BLDC uses three cheap Hall sensors and six switching steps – some torque ripple. PMSM uses an encoder and feeds smooth sine currents 120° apart – very smooth torque. The SPM/IPM drawings just show where magnets can sit.

Emphasise
  • Electronic commutation needs the rotor angle.
  • Misconception: brushless motors are 'AC motors' with no relation to DC – they are electronically commutated DC motors.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why 120°? A: Three phases spaced evenly around one electrical period.

Transition: Two more motor types complete the picture.

Slide 15Induction motor and stepper motor~10 min

Induction motor: the stator makes a rotating field; it induces currents in the rotor bars, which then feel a force – no magnets at all. The rotor must turn a bit slower than the field, otherwise nothing is induced: that is slip. Work the example: 50 Hz, 2 pole pairs, 1500 rpm synchronous, at 1440 rpm slip is 4 %. Stepper: each pulse moves one step, 1.8°. The controller counts steps and assumes the motor followed – fine until the load is too heavy and steps are lost silently.

Emphasise
  • Slip is necessary for induction torque.
  • Misconception: a stepper always knows its position – it only assumes it.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why do 3D printers lose layers? A: Lost steps when the nozzle hits something.

Transition: Let us put all types side by side.

Slide 16Comparison of machine types~7 min

Read row by row and let students guess the green and red cells before revealing. Key points: PMSM has the best torque per size and smoothness, induction the highest speed and robustness, stepper needs no sensor but is jerky and slow. Brushed DC only needs a simple drive. The rule of thumb summarises industrial practice.

Emphasise
  • Choice depends on the application.
  • Misconception: 'best motor' exists independent of application.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Which for an electric bike? A: Usually a BLDC hub motor: cheap, efficient, brushless.

Transition: Check yourselves.

Slide 17Session 1.2 – Check your understanding~8 min

Answers: (1) The commutator switches each coil's current as the rotor turns so torque keeps the same direction; in a BLDC the drive electronics do this, using Hall sensors to know the rotor angle. (2) Its windings – the heat source – are on the stator, touching the housing; in a brushed motor the heat is in the spinning rotor. (3) No; a stepper is open loop – if the load exceeded the available torque, steps were lost and nothing measures it.

Emphasise
  • Connect answers to where the current flows.
  • Misconception: Hall sensors measure speed precisely – they give only six positions per electrical turn.

Transition: Summary.

Slide 18Session 1.2 – Summary and key terms~7 min

Every machine has a stator, rotor and air gap. The coil currents must be switched as the rotor turns – that is commutation, done by brushes or by electronics with a sensor. Brushless motors run cooler and do not wear. Induction needs slip; steppers count steps and can lose them. Go through the key terms and the three remembered formulas. Use remaining minutes for questions.

Emphasise
  • Commutation is the unifying idea.
  • Slip and lost steps as typical exam questions.

Transition: After the break: equations for the DC motor.

Session 1.3 · Electrical and mechanical models Curriculum code 1.3 · 60 min · slides 19–29

Slide 20Refresher: Newton's law for rotation~6 min

Students know F = m·a. For rotation the same law reads T = J·dω/dt: torque replaces force, moment of inertia replaces mass. Use the playground roundabout: hard to start, hard to stop. Show the table, then the example: 0.5 N·m on 1e-4 kg·m² gives 5000 rad/s², so 3000 rpm in 63 ms. Emphasise the net torque: motor minus friction minus load.

Emphasise
  • Net torque determines acceleration, not speed.
  • Misconception: constant torque gives constant speed – it gives constant acceleration.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What is J for a disc? A: ½·m·r².

Transition: Now the motor's electrical side.

Slide 21Equivalent circuit of the DC motor~5 min

The electrical side is the RL circuit students know from the lab, plus a voltage source e = k_e·ω that grows with speed and pushes against the supply. On the right the mechanical side: inertia, friction, motor torque, load torque. Read the symbol table and units aloud. Sign convention: voltage and current positive into the motor, load torque opposes motion.

Emphasise
  • Back-EMF is an opposing source.
  • Misconception: the motor is 'just a resistor' – then speed would not matter.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why include L? A: It makes the current change gradually, not instantly.

Transition: Write the equations.

Slide 22The two motor equations~7 min

KVL around the loop gives the electrical equation; Newton for rotation gives the mechanical one. They are linked: current makes torque, speed makes back-EMF. Two first-order ODEs together make a second-order system. The key box shows energy conservation: power converted electrically, e·i, must equal mechanical T·ω, so k_t = k_e. Show the datasheet conversion: divide V per 1000 rpm by 104.7.

Emphasise
  • Two coupled first-order ODEs.
  • Misconception: k_t and k_e are independent design choices.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Where does 104.7 come from? A: 1000 rpm = 1000·2π/60 = 104.7 rad/s.

Transition: Friction deserves a closer look.

Slide 23Friction and load torque~5 min

Use the heavy box: hard to start moving, easier once sliding – that is stiction then Coulomb friction. Viscous friction grows with speed like stirring honey; it is the only linear part and the one we keep in today's model. Load torque is what the job demands, for example gravity on a vertical axis, which is present even at standstill.

Emphasise
  • Today: T_f ≈ Bω only.
  • Misconception: friction is always proportional to speed.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why does stiction cause problems? A: The axis sticks, then jumps – small position errors.

Transition: What happens at constant speed?

Slide 24Steady state: the speed–torque line~6 min

At constant speed all derivatives are zero, and the equations give a straight line. Work the two end points with the class: no load, 48/0.1 = 480 rad/s; stalled, 48 A and 4.8 N·m. In between speed droops linearly with load. Lower voltage shifts the line down – this is how voltage controls speed.

Emphasise
  • Set d/dt = 0 for steady state.
  • Misconception: stall is a safe operating point – 48 A would burn the motor.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why does speed drop under load? A: More current is needed, more voltage drops across R, less is left for back-EMF.

Transition: Draw the equations as a picture.

Slide 25Block diagram of the DC motor~6 min

A block diagram is a picture of the equations: arrows are signals, boxes act on them, circles add or subtract. Trace the path: voltage minus back-EMF drives the RL circuit, current times k_t is torque, minus load torque accelerates the inertia, integrating speed gives angle. The back-EMF path is a built-in feedback: faster means less current. Laplace boxes are only flagged for Day 2.

Emphasise
  • Feedback loop inside the motor itself.
  • Misconception: block diagrams are new physics – they are the same equations.

Transition: Before solving, a refresher on first-order ODEs.

Slide 26Refresher: first-order ODEs and the exponential response~6 min

Everyone has seen RC charging: fast at first, then slower. The solution is y = y∞(1 − e^(−t/τ)). Memorise 63 % after one τ, 95 % after three. τ = RC or L/R. Example: L = 0.5 mH, R = 1 Ω gives 0.5 ms. Point out the dashed initial slope reaches the final value at exactly τ.

Emphasise
  • One energy store, one τ, one exponential.
  • Misconception: the response 'finishes' at t = τ – it is only 63 %.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: How long is 'settled'? A: Engineers say 3–5 τ.

Transition: Apply it to the motor.

Slide 27The motor's two time constants: worked example~8 min

The motor has two energy stores, so two time constants. Go through the steps: τe = 0.5 ms, τm = 10 ms, final speed about 240 rad/s. Show the plot: speed rises like an exponential with τm, while current shoots up within a millisecond to about 21 A, because at standstill there is no back-EMF. As speed rises, back-EMF pulls current down. Larger load inertia makes τm larger.

Emphasise
  • τe ≪ τm: current fast, speed slow.
  • Misconception: start current equals running current.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why don't real drives blow fuses? A: They limit current electronically.

Transition: Check yourselves.

Slide 28Session 1.3 – Check your understanding~7 min

Answers: (1) 95 % after about 3τm = 60 ms. (2) τm = RJ/(k_t k_e) is proportional to J, so it doubles. (3) At switch-on ω = 0, so no back-EMF; only R limits the current, i ≈ v/R. As the motor speeds up, back-EMF grows and the current falls.

Emphasise
  • Link Q3 back to the step-response plot.
  • Misconception: inductance limits the current permanently – only for ~τe.

Transition: Summary.

Slide 29Session 1.3 – Summary and key terms~4 min

Motor = RL circuit with back-EMF, coupled to an inertia. Energy conservation gives k_t = k_e. At constant speed we get the straight speed–torque line. Two time constants: fast electrical, slow mechanical. Read the key terms.

Emphasise
  • The two equations are the core of the day.

Transition: After the break: gears and screws.

Session 1.4 · Actuators and motion transmission Curriculum code 1.4 · 45 min · slides 30–40

Slide 31Motion transmission options~4 min

Most machines need linear motion or a slower, stronger rotation. Go through the table: ball screws are stiff and precise, belts fast but springy, racks for long travel, gearboxes for torque, direct drives with no mechanism at all. The two boxes summarise the trade-off.

Emphasise
  • Every transmission trades something.
  • Misconception: direct drive is always better – it needs a much bigger motor.

Transition: First the direct option.

Slide 32Linear actuators: linear motor and voice coil~4 min

A linear motor is a PMSM cut open and rolled flat. A voice coil is a loudspeaker: coil in a magnet gap, short stroke, extremely fast. The force law is F = k_f·i – exactly the F = B·l·i from this morning with N turns. Equations are the DC motor's with force, velocity and mass.

Emphasise
  • Same physics, linear version.
  • Misconception: linear motors need a screw.

Transition: Now gears – with a bicycle.

Slide 33Refresher: gear ratio – think bicycle gears~6 min

In a low bicycle gear you pedal fast and easily while the wheel turns slowly with a big push. Gear ratio N = teeth of the big gear over the small one = motor speed over load speed. Speed goes down by N, torque goes up by N, power stays the same. Work the example: N = 3, 3000 rpm becomes 1000 rpm, 1 N·m becomes 3 N·m, 314 W on both sides.

Emphasise
  • Speed ÷ N, torque × N.
  • Misconception: a gearbox creates power.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What about losses? A: Multiply output torque by efficiency η.

Transition: Real gearboxes for servo axes.

Slide 34Gearboxes: planetary and harmonic drives~4 min

Planetary: sun in the middle, planets around, ring outside – compact and efficient, ratio up to 10 per stage. Example formula with ring 60 and sun 20 gives N = 4. Harmonic drive: a flexible cup squeezed into an oval gives 50–160 in one stage with almost no play – robot joints. Worm gears can be self-locking but are inefficient.

Emphasise
  • Planetary for servo axes, harmonic for robots.

Transition: Turning rotation into straight-line motion.

Slide 35Ball screw~4 min

Analogy: nut on a bolt. One turn moves the nut by the lead p. So v = p·ω/2π. Example: 10 mm lead at 3000 rpm = 50 rev/s gives 0.5 m/s. Balls roll in the thread, so friction is low and efficiency around 90 %.

Emphasise
  • Lead = travel per revolution.
  • Misconception: forgetting 2π when using rad/s.

Transition: What does the motor 'feel' through all this?

Slide 36Reflected inertia~6 min

Back to the bicycle: in a low gear it feels light to accelerate. The motor sees the load inertia divided by N squared. One factor N because the load turns slower, another because torque is multiplied. Derive it from equal kinetic energy. Example: 9e-4 divided by 9 is 1e-4. The table gives the screw and belt versions.

Emphasise
  • Inertia ÷ N².
  • Misconception: inertia divides by N, not N².
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why m(p/2π)² for a screw? A: Same energy argument with v = (p/2π)ω.

Transition: So which ratio is best?

Slide 37Choosing the gear ratio~5 min

Too small a ratio: the motor fights the whole load. Too large: the motor spins very fast and spends torque accelerating itself. The curves peak where reflected load inertia equals motor inertia: N_opt = √(J_L/J_m). Example 16 gives 4. Note the flat peak – and that in practice maximum motor speed often decides.

Emphasise
  • Inertia matching.
  • Rule: inertia ratio below about 3–10.

Transition: Real transmissions are not ideal.

Slide 38Real transmissions: losses, play and springiness~4 min

Three imperfections. Efficiency: some torque lost to friction. Backlash: when direction reverses, the motor turns a little before the load follows – the plot shows the dead zone. Stiffness: belts and shafts twist like springs, which leads to resonance in session 2.1.

Emphasise
  • Backlash matters at reversal.
  • Misconception: steel parts are perfectly rigid.

Transition: Check.

Slide 39Session 1.4 – Check your understanding~5 min

Answers: (1) 2500/5 = 500 rpm; torque 0.4 × 5 = 2 N·m. (2) 0.05/25 = 0.002 kg·m². (3) At reversal the gear teeth must cross the gap before they touch the other flank; during that time the motor moves but the load does not, giving position error.

Emphasise
  • Q2 tests the N² rule.
  • Misconception: dividing inertia by 5.

Transition: Summary.

Slide 40Session 1.4 – Summary and key terms~3 min

Gears and screws trade speed for torque or force; the load appears at the motor divided by N squared; the best acceleration is at inertia matching; linear motors follow the same equations. Read the key terms.

Emphasise
  • Speed ÷ N, torque × N, inertia ÷ N².

Transition: Next: is the motor big enough?

Session 1.5 · Characteristics and selection criteria Curriculum code 1.5 · 45 min · slides 41–49

Slide 42Servo motor torque–speed envelope~5 min

Sprint versus jog: the blue zone is what the motor can do forever, the cream zone only for seconds. Peak torque is limited by current, the top-right slope by voltage, because back-EMF eats the supply voltage at high speed. Point out the rated point: 1.27 N·m at 3000 rpm, our 400 W motor.

Emphasise
  • Two zones: continuous and short-time.
  • Misconception: peak torque is usable continuously.

Transition: Why is the continuous zone limited? Heat.

Slide 43Thermal limits~6 min

Heat flows like current: losses are the source, thermal resistance to ambient, thermal capacitance storing heat. The temperature rises exactly like RC charging. Copper loss goes with current squared, so double torque gives four times the heat – the curves cross the limit quickly. Example: 50 W through 1.6 K/W is 80 K rise. Because τth is minutes, short overloads are fine.

Emphasise
  • Heat ∝ T².
  • Misconception: temperature follows load instantly.

Transition: How do we judge a whole cycle?

Slide 44Duty cycle and RMS torque~6 min

Because heat goes with torque squared and the motor heats slowly, it feels the average of T² over the cycle. The square root of that is the RMS torque. Explain why the plain average fails: braking torque is negative but heats just as much. Also check peak torque and maximum speed, and remember holding torque on vertical axes.

Emphasise
  • RMS ≤ continuous, peak ≤ peak.
  • Misconception: average torque can be used.

Transition: Full worked example.

Slide 45Worked sizing example (1/2)~6 min

Go step by step, units at every line. Speed: 0.5 m/s with a 10 mm lead is 50 rev/s, 3000 rpm, 314 rad/s. Acceleration in 0.1 s: 3140 rad/s². Table inertia via the screw formula: 1.27e-4. Add screw and coupling: 2.37e-4. Note the d⁴ in the screw inertia.

Emphasise
  • Numbers are round on purpose.
  • Misconception: forgetting the screw's own inertia.

Transition: Now torques and checks.

Slide 46Worked sizing example (2/2)~7 min

Pick a 400 W candidate and add its inertia. Torque per segment is J·α plus or minus friction: 0.92, 0.06, −0.81, 0. RMS over the one-second cycle: 0.39 N·m. Check peak, RMS and speed – all inside the envelope plot, three times thermal margin.

Emphasise
  • Every point inside the envelope.
  • Misconception: only peak torque matters.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Could a smaller motor work? A: Thermally yes, but the inertia ratio becomes large and tuning harder.

Transition: Finally, match the drive.

Slide 47Motor–drive matching and checklist~6 min

At top speed the supply must exceed the back-EMF plus the resistive drop. Example: k_e = 0.3, 314 rad/s gives 94 V – a 48 V drive cannot reach that speed, a 230 V drive can. Peak current is peak torque over k_t: about 3 A. Walk through the eight-step checklist.

Emphasise
  • Voltage sets speed, current sets torque.
  • Misconception: any drive works with any motor.

Transition: Check.

Slide 48Session 1.5 – Check your understanding~6 min

Answers: (1) Heat goes with torque squared: 3× torque gives 9× losses; the winding would overheat after seconds to minutes. (2) T_rms = √(1² × 0.1 / 1) = 0.32 N·m. (3) Heating depends on T², so negative torques heat too and peaks count more; the average would underestimate heating and could even be zero.

Emphasise
  • Q2: show the square root step explicitly.

Transition: Summary.

Slide 49Session 1.5 – Summary and key terms~3 min

Two zones, exponential heating, RMS for heat, peak for acceleration, and a fixed order of sizing steps. Read the key terms.

Emphasise
  • Sizing order: speed → inertia → torque → RMS/peak → drive.

Transition: Last block: modelling in general.

Session 2.1 · Modelling of dynamic systems – fundamental physical models (part 1) + day wrap-up Curriculum code 2.1 · 60 min · slides 50–63

Slide 51Lumped-parameter modelling~4 min

Modelling recipe: inputs and outputs, ideal elements, free-body diagram or KVL, element laws, ODE, compare with measurement. The table shows the three element types in three domains. Each energy store adds one order. Keep the model as simple as the question allows.

Emphasise
  • Simplest model that answers the question.
  • Misconception: more detail is always better.

Transition: The classic example.

Slide 52Mass–spring–damper~8 min

Car suspension: spring carries the car, shock absorber kills bouncing. Draw the free-body diagram: spring force kx and damper force b·ẋ to the left, F to the right. Newton gives m·ẍ + b·ẋ + k·x = F. Two numbers summarise it: natural frequency – how fast it bounces – and damping ratio – how fast bouncing dies. Example: 316 rad/s, about 50 Hz, ζ ≈ 0.1: lightly damped.

Emphasise
  • ωn and ζ describe every second-order system.
  • Misconception: signs – both spring and damper oppose motion.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Units of ζ? A: None; it is a ratio.

Transition: What does ζ do to a step response?

Slide 53Second-order step responses~7 min

Apply a sudden constant force. With ζ = 0.1 the mass overshoots 73 % and rings – worn shock absorbers. With 0.7 it is fast with 4 % overshoot – a common design target. With ζ ≥ 1 no overshoot but sluggish. Show the overshoot formula; poles are flagged for Day 2.

Emphasise
  • ζ ≈ 0.7 as design target.
  • Misconception: more damping is always better – it slows the response.

Transition: The rotating version.

Slide 54Rotational mechanical systems~5 min

Same equation with J, B and k_θ. A shaft twists like a spring; work the steel shaft example: 785 N·m/rad. Couplings and belts are springs too, and the softest one dominates. Without load the spring term disappears.

Emphasise
  • Translation ↔ rotation substitution.
  • Misconception: shafts are infinitely stiff.

Transition: And the electrical version.

Slide 55Electrical RLC network~5 min

KVL with charge q gives L·q̈ + R·q̇ + q/C = v – the same form as the mass–spring–damper. Inductance is mass, resistance damper, 1/C spring. Example filter: 232 Hz, ζ = 0.03 – it rings a lot.

Emphasise
  • Same ODE, different letters.
  • Misconception: R is like the spring.

Transition: Put the analogies in one table.

Slide 56Analogies and the motor as a link~6 min

Read the table across: force, torque, voltage; velocity, speed, current; and so on. Push times flow always equals power. The motor is the bridge between electrical and mechanical worlds: T = k·i and e = k·ω, converting power without storing it. Two stores, L and J, give second order. Lagrange and state space are 'going further'.

Emphasise
  • Effort × flow = power.
  • Misconception: analogies are only a memory trick – they make the maths identical.

Transition: A preview of trouble ahead: springy shafts.

Slide 57Preview: two-inertia system~6 min

Motor and load connected by a springy shaft. Like a playground swing: push at the right rhythm and it swings higher – resonance. The plot shows how strongly the motor moves when shaken by a sine torque of each frequency: a dip at 113 Hz and a peak at 225 Hz, unlike the rigid shaft's straight line. This peak limits how fast we can tune a servo later in the course.

Emphasise
  • Resonance limits servo speed.
  • Misconception: a stiffer controller fixes everything.

Transition: Final check.

Slide 58Session 2.1 – Check your understanding~7 min

Answers: (1) ωn = √(k/m), so 4× stiffness doubles ωn. (2) The inductance L plays the mass, the resistance R plays the damper. (3) Increase b: ζ = b/(2√(km)) rises, and higher ζ means less overshoot.

Emphasise
  • Q1: square root, not linear.
  • Misconception: overshoot is fixed by a stiffer spring.

Transition: Summary.

Slide 59Session 2.1 – Summary and key terms~3 min

One second-order ODE with ωn and ζ describes the mass–spring–damper, the shaft and the RLC circuit. Low damping means ringing. The motor links domains, and springy shafts create resonance. Read the key terms.

Emphasise
  • ωn, ζ as the two key numbers.

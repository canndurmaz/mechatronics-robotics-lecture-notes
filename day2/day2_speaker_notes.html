Session 2.1 · Nonlinear effects and linearisation Curriculum code 2.1 · 30 min

Slide 2Agenda and learning objectives~3 min

Good morning. Today we go from physical models to control. Most of today's tools are new for you: Laplace transform, Bode plots, feedback and PID. Whenever we need maths you have not seen yet, a blue Refresher box explains it first. Grey 'Going further' text is optional; skip it on first reading. Read the five objectives aloud: by tonight you should be able to turn an ODE into a transfer function, read a Bode plot, explain PID with a cruise control and judge stability.

Emphasise
  • Refresher boxes fill the gaps; Going-further text is optional.
  • Misconception: 'control = PID tuning by trial'. Today shows why models matter.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Do we need yesterday's motor equations? A: Yes, the DC motor returns in 2.2 and in the cascade slide.

Transition: Let us start with what makes real machines nonlinear.

Slide 4What “nonlinear” means: limits, gaps and play~5 min

Linear means: double the input, double the output. Use everyday pictures: a gas pedal already on the floor cannot give more (saturation); a loose steering wheel does nothing for small turns (dead-zone); gear teeth have a gap, so the load does not move until the gap is closed (backlash). Magnetic saturation is like a full sponge: more current gives less extra flux, so the inductance drops. Show the backlash picture and let students guess what happens when the motor reverses.

Emphasise
  • Every real axis has limits and play.
  • Misconception: 'nonlinear' means 'complicated maths'. Here it just means the straight-line rule breaks.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Can we just ignore these effects? A: For small signals around a working point often yes; that is exactly linearisation.

Transition: A fourth, very common nonlinearity is friction.

Slide 5Friction: hard to start, easier to keep moving~4 min

Ask who has moved a heavy wardrobe: starting needs a big push, keeping it sliding needs less. That is breakaway (static) friction versus Coulomb friction. Viscous friction grows with speed and is linear. At zero speed the force jumps, so there is no slope and no tangent: we cannot linearise there. In machines this gives stick-slip, jerky motion at low speed, and hunting around the target. The practical fix is to add the expected friction force as feedforward.

Emphasise
  • At v = 0 friction has no slope: it cannot be linearised.
  • Misconception: friction is just a damper. Only the viscous part is.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why does hunting happen? A: The integrator builds up force until breakaway, the axis jumps past the target, and the cycle repeats.

Transition: How do we handle the smooth nonlinearities? With a tangent line.

Slide 6Linearisation: zoom in until the curve looks straight~5 min

This is the one idea of the session. Zoom into any smooth curve and it looks like a straight line: the tangent. That is the first-order Taylor series from calculus. Walk through the example: f = x squared at x0 = 3 has slope 6. At 3.1 the tangent gives 9.6, the truth is 9.61. At 4 the error is already 6 percent. So the linear model is local. We describe small deviations delta x from the operating point. For several variables the slopes become matrices; that is Going further.

Emphasise
  • Linear model = tangent at the operating point.
  • Misconception: a linearised model works everywhere. It is only valid nearby.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: How small is 'small'? A: No fixed number; compare linear and true answers, as in the example.

Transition: Let us apply this to a pendulum.

Slide 7Example 1: pendulum / unbalanced arm~5 min

An arm on a motor shaft is a pendulum. Only sin theta is nonlinear; its slope is cos theta. Hanging down, cos is plus one and gravity acts like a spring: a stable swing at 5.7 rad/s for a 30 cm arm. Turned upside down, cos is minus one: a negative spring, and it falls over. Same equation, opposite behaviour, only because the operating point changed. The plot shows the tangent lines at 0 and 180 degrees; sin theta is within 5 percent of theta up to about 30 degrees.

Emphasise
  • Stability can depend on the operating point.
  • Misconception: one model of a machine is valid for all positions.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Which real axes behave like this? A: Robot arms and anything moving against gravity.

Transition: A second example with a magnet.

Slide 8Example 2: electromagnet~4 min

Use the broom-balancing picture: the closer the iron gets to the magnet, the stronger the pull, so any small error grows. Go through the four steps: weight equals magnet force at the operating point; the two slopes give K_z = 392 N/m and K_i = 1.96 N/A; the poles are plus and minus 62.6 per second, one of them in the right half-plane, so it is unstable on its own; and the slopes change with the gap, so the model is local. Poles are explained properly in the next session.

Emphasise
  • Negative stiffness means instability without feedback.
  • Misconception: unstable systems cannot be controlled. Feedback can stabilise them.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Where is this used? A: Maglev, magnetic bearings, solenoid valves.

Transition: Time to check your understanding.

Slide 9Session 2.1 – Check your understanding~2 min

Let students discuss in pairs for two minutes, then collect answers. Answers: 1) Coulomb friction jumps at v = 0; there is no unique slope, so no tangent exists. 2) The term mgl cos theta0 changes sign: hanging it acts like a spring that pulls back, inverted like a spring that pushes away. 3) Not directly: K_z = 2mg/z0 halves and the needed current changes, so we must re-linearise at 10 mm.

Emphasise
  • Answers given orally, not on the slide.
  • Watch for students who think linearisation removes nonlinearity everywhere.

Transition: Let us summarise.

Slide 10Session 2.1 – Summary and key terms~2 min

Recap the four take-aways and read the key terms. Stress that from now on we assume a linear model around an operating point, and that jumps such as friction need compensation rather than linearisation. Ask one student to explain 'operating point' in their own words.

Emphasise
  • Linear models are local approximations.
  • Misconception: 'deviation' means error; here it means distance from the operating point.

Transition: Next: how to write linear models compactly.

Session 2.2 · Transfer functions and state space Curriculum code 2.2 · 60 min

Slide 12Refresher: complex numbers and the complex plane~5 min

Students know complex numbers, but refresh the polar view because Bode plots depend on it. Draw 3+4j: magnitude 5, angle 53 degrees. Key rule: when multiplying, magnitudes multiply and angles add; when dividing, they divide and subtract. Work 1/(1+j) on the board: 0.707 at minus 45 degrees. Finally e to the st with s = sigma + j omega: sigma decides growth or decay, omega the oscillation. This is why poles are plotted in a complex plane.

Emphasise
  • Polar form: magnitude and angle.
  • Misconception: j is 'imaginary' so it has no physical meaning; here it encodes phase.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why j instead of i? A: Electrical engineers use i for current.

Transition: With this, the Laplace transform becomes a simple tool.

Slide 13Refresher: the Laplace transform as a tool~7 min

Present Laplace as a translator, like logarithms turning multiplication into addition: it turns derivatives into multiplication by s. We will only use the table, not the integral. Work the RC circuit step by step: ODE, replace v-dot by sV, collect terms, divide. The result 1/(RCs+1) is the transfer function. For a step input multiply by 1/s and use the table to get back the familiar charging curve. Students have seen this curve before, so they can check the tool gives the known answer.

Emphasise
  • Derivative becomes multiplication by s.
  • Misconception: s is a frequency in Hz. It is a complex variable.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why zero initial conditions? A: The transfer function describes input-output only; initial conditions add a separate free response.

Transition: The ratio we just found has a name: the transfer function.

Slide 14Transfer function, poles and zeros~5 min

The transfer function is the system's recipe: output over input. Poles are the roots of the denominator, zeros the roots of the numerator. Poles decide stability and speed, which is the most important fact of today. The order equals the number of energy stores. The DC gain G(0) is what you get after everything settles. Do the example: zero at minus 2, poles at minus 1 and minus 3, DC gain 2/3. Point to the plane: left half is the stable side.

Emphasise
  • Poles = roots of the denominator; left half-plane = stable.
  • Misconception: zeros decide stability. They do not.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What does a pole 'look like' physically? A: A natural motion e^(pt) of the system.

Transition: The simplest case has one pole.

Slide 15First-order system: one energy store~4 min

A cup of coffee cooling or an RC circuit charging: one energy store, one pole. After one time constant we are at 63 percent, after four at about 98 percent, and it never overshoots. Show the initial slope line reaching the final value at t = tau. Motor winding examples: 2 mH and 1 ohm give 2 ms. A flywheel with friction has tau = J/B.

Emphasise
  • 63 % at t = tau; no overshoot ever.
  • Misconception: the system is 'done' after one tau. It needs about 4 tau.

Transition: With two energy stores the response can oscillate.

Slide 16Second-order system: the car suspension~6 min

Use the car suspension: a mass on a spring with a shock absorber. Natural frequency says how fast it bounces, damping ratio says how quickly the bouncing dies out. Worn shock absorbers mean small zeta and a bouncy ride. Zeta around 0.7 is fast with about 5 percent overshoot. Do the quarter-car numbers: omega_n = sqrt(16000/250) = 8 rad/s, zeta = 2000/(2*2000) = 0.5. Point to the curves for each zeta.

Emphasise
  • omega_n = speed, zeta = bounciness.
  • Misconception: more damping is always better. zeta > 1 is slow.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why 0.7? A: Good compromise between speed and overshoot.

Transition: These behaviours can be read directly from the pole positions.

Slide 17Where the poles are tells you how the system moves~4 min

Walk through the map: the further left, the faster the motion dies out; the higher up, the faster it wiggles; on the right side it grows. The imaginary axis is the border: sustained oscillation. The slowest poles dominate: with poles at minus 2 and minus 20 the response settles in about 4/2 = 2 s. This picture is the most useful mental model of the whole day.

Emphasise
  • Real part = decay rate, imaginary part = oscillation.
  • Misconception: a pole far from the origin on the imaginary axis is stable. It oscillates forever.

Transition: State space uses matrices, so a quick refresher.

Slide 18Refresher: matrices for state space~5 min

Do the matrix-vector multiplication slowly: row times column. Then show why we need it: two coupled ODEs fit into one line x-dot = A x. Eigenvalues are found from det(lambda I - A) = 0; for this 2x2 the steps give lambda squared + 2 lambda + 4 and the roots minus 1 plus/minus j1.73. Stress the link: eigenvalues of A are exactly the poles. The matrix is a mass-spring-damper with m=1, k=4, c=2.

Emphasise
  • Eigenvalues of A = poles.
  • Misconception: matrices are a different theory. Same system, compact notation.

Transition: Now the general state-space form.

Slide 19State-space representation~5 min

The state is what the system 'remembers': positions, speeds, currents, capacitor voltages. Show how the mass-spring-damper becomes A, B, C, D: x1-dot = x2 and x2-dot from Newton. In the block diagram each integrator stores one state. State space handles several inputs and outputs and is the basis for observers on Day 3. The formula linking it to G(s) is Going further.

Emphasise
  • One state per energy store / integrator.
  • Misconception: there is only one correct state choice. Many choices give the same G(s).

Transition: Let us write the DC motor both ways.

Slide 20The DC motor in both representations~5 min

Two equations from things students know: KVL for the winding and Newton for the rotor. Two energy stores, current in the inductance and speed of the inertia, so two states. Laplace-transform both, eliminate the current, and you get the transfer function. The k_t k_e term is back-EMF: when the motor speeds up it generates a voltage that reduces the current. That is a built-in feedback loop. Load torque is a second input, a disturbance.

Emphasise
  • Back-EMF is natural feedback.
  • Misconception: the motor speed is simply proportional to voltage at all times; only in steady state.

Transition: Now with real numbers.

Slide 21DC motor: worked numerical example~4 min

Go through the four numbered steps. Characteristic polynomial s^2 + 500 s + 20 860 gives poles at minus 46 and minus 454: a slow mechanical and a fast electrical pole. DC gain about 10 rad/s per volt, so 24 V gives about 240 rad/s, settling in about 4/46 = 87 ms. Point to the current plot: 20 A at the start, limited only by resistance. This is why drives need current limits.

Emphasise
  • Electrical pole much faster than mechanical.
  • Misconception: starting current equals rated current.

Transition: Systems are built from blocks; here are the rules.

Slide 22Block-diagram algebra~4 min

Series blocks multiply, parallel blocks add, a negative feedback loop gives G1/(1+G1G2). Do the key-box numbers: with G1 = 10 the loop gain is 0.91, doubling G1 changes it only to 0.95. This is the main reason feedback is used: the result depends little on the uncertain plant. Mason's rule is Going further.

Emphasise
  • Feedback formula G1/(1+G1G2).
  • Misconception: feedback reduces gain, so it is wasteful. It buys insensitivity.

Transition: Check your understanding.

Slide 23Session 2.2 – Check your understanding~3 min

Pairs, two minutes. Answers: 1) Stable, both poles in the left half-plane; the pole at minus 2 dominates; settling about 4/2 = 2 s. 2) Same oscillation frequency but slower decay: more overshoot and longer ringing. 3) Two independent energy stores: magnetic energy in the inductance (current) and kinetic energy in the inertia (speed).

Emphasise
  • Link every answer to the pole map.
  • Misconception: the faster pole dominates.

Transition: Summary.

Slide 24Session 2.2 – Summary and key terms~3 min

Recap: Laplace turns ODEs into algebra, poles decide stability and speed, first and second order cover most sub-systems, eigenvalues of A are the poles, and feedback makes the result insensitive to the plant. Read the key terms and ask a student to define time constant.

Emphasise
  • Poles are the thread connecting every slide.
  • Misconception: transfer function and state space are different systems.

Transition: After the break: getting models from measurements.

Session 2.3 · System identification and frequency response Curriculum code 2.3 · 60 min

Slide 26Where do models come from?~4 min

Three ways: physics (white box), physics plus measured parameters (grey box) and pure curve fitting (black box). In industry grey box is the default: we know the motor equations but measure inertia and friction. Go through the five workflow steps and stress the last one: check the model on new data. Ljung's quote: a model is never true, only useful. Modern servo drives have the signal analyser built in.

Emphasise
  • Grey box = physics + measurement.
  • Misconception: a model that fits the data used to build it is validated.

Transition: The quickest test is a step.

Slide 27Step test: first order plus dead time~6 min

Use the shower analogy for dead time: turn the tap and nothing changes for a few seconds. Then the four steps: K from the final change, read the 28 and 63 percent times, tau = 1.5 times the difference = 1.29 s, theta = 1.86 - 1.29 = 0.57 s. Note that the dead time also hides the extra small lags of the real plant. Good for thermal and process loops, too coarse for springy mechanics.

Emphasise
  • Four reading steps, units at every step.
  • Misconception: dead time is a measuring error. It is real delay.

Transition: If the response overshoots, we fit a second-order model.

Slide 28Step test: underdamped second-order system~5 min

Read overshoot and peak time from the plot. The formula gives zeta from the overshoot alone: 25 percent gives 0.40. Peak time gives the ringing frequency pi/t_p = 78.5 rad/s, and dividing by sqrt(1 - zeta^2) gives omega_n = 86 rad/s. Remind students to use overshoot as a fraction, not a percentage, in the logarithm.

Emphasise
  • Overshoot gives zeta; peak time gives omega_n.
  • Misconception: using 25 instead of 0.25 in ln.

Transition: Next we test with sines instead of steps; first a refresher.

Slide 29Refresher: sinusoidal frequency response~7 min

Let a student shake a ruler slowly and quickly: slow, the tip follows; fast, it moves less and lags. A linear system driven by a sine always answers with a sine of the same frequency, only scaled and shifted. The scaling is |G(j omega)|, the shift is the angle. Recipe: replace s by j omega and use the complex-number rules from 2.2. Do both examples for 1/(s+1): 0.707 at minus 45 degrees at 1 rad/s, 0.0995 at minus 84 degrees at 10 rad/s.

Emphasise
  • Same frequency out, different amplitude and phase.
  • Misconception: the output frequency changes. Not for linear systems.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What if the output has other frequencies? A: Then the system is nonlinear or noisy; coherence will show it.

Transition: Gains span many orders of magnitude, so we use decibels.

Slide 30Refresher: decibels and logarithmic scales~5 min

Gains go from 0.001 to 1000; a log scale shows both. Decibel = 20 log10 of the gain. Learn a few anchors: 10 is 20 dB, 2 is 6 dB, 0.707 is minus 3 dB, 0.1 is minus 20 dB. Gains in series multiply, so dB add. On the log frequency axis each decade, a factor 10, takes the same space. Example: 0.05 is minus 26 dB.

Emphasise
  • Anchor values: x10 = 20 dB, x2 = 6 dB.
  • Misconception: 0 dB means zero output. It means gain 1.

Transition: Now we can read a Bode plot.

Slide 31How to read a Bode plot~8 min

A Bode plot is the frequency response for all frequencies: magnitude in dB on top, phase below, both against log frequency. Do the reading exercise at 0.1 rad/s: 40 dB means gain 100, phase about minus 85 degrees. Then the straight-line rules: each pole bends the magnitude down by 20 dB per decade and costs 90 degrees; each zero does the opposite; an integrator is minus 20 dB per decade everywhere. Show how the example follows: integrator, zero at 1 flattens, pole at 10 bends down.

Emphasise
  • Read magnitude, convert dB, read phase.
  • Misconception: the phase plot is optional. Stability depends on it.

Transition: Mechanical systems often show a resonance peak.

Slide 32Resonance: pushing a swing~5 min

Pushing a swing at its own rhythm gives large motion with small pushes: resonance. Below omega_n the output follows; at omega_n the peak is 1/(2 zeta) and the phase is minus 90; above it falls 40 dB per decade. With zeta = 0.05 the peak is a factor 10, plus 20 dB, typical of belts and couplings. Exact peak formulas are Going further.

Emphasise
  • Peak height 1/(2 zeta).
  • Misconception: resonance only matters in bridges. Every servo axis has some.

Transition: How do we measure such a plot on a machine?

Slide 33Measuring a frequency response on a servo axis~7 min

The drive adds a test signal while the axis is held in closed loop, which is safe and handles gravity. Show the three signal types: chirp sweeps frequency, PRBS looks random and covers all frequencies at once, stepped sine is slow but clean. The amplitude must overcome friction but stay below current limits. Averaging several runs reduces noise. Cross-spectra are Going further.

Emphasise
  • Test in closed loop, choose the amplitude carefully.
  • Misconception: bigger excitation is always better; it can saturate.

Transition: Here is a typical measured result.

Slide 34Two-mass axis: coherence and model error~7 min

Motor and load connected by a springy shaft. Up to about 50 Hz the rigid model fits; near the anti-resonance at 130 Hz and resonance at 260 Hz it is completely wrong. Coherence near 1 means the measurement is trustworthy; trust it only above about 0.8. Conclusion: keep control bandwidth below the first resonance, or add notch filters on Day 3.

Emphasise
  • Simple models fail near resonances.
  • Misconception: a low-coherence dip is a real feature of the plant.

Transition: Check your understanding.

Slide 35Session 2.3 – Check your understanding~3 min

Answers: 1) 0.1 is minus 20 dB, 1000 is plus 60 dB. 2) Gain 2 is plus 6 dB; a quarter period lag is minus 90 degrees. 3) Near the resonance the real plant has much more gain and different phase than the model, so a loop designed there can become unstable.

Emphasise
  • Use the dB anchor table.
  • Misconception: lag of a quarter period equals minus 25 degrees.

Transition: Summary.

Slide 36Session 2.3 – Summary and key terms~3 min

Recap the five take-aways and the key terms. Emphasise that a Bode plot is just the sine test repeated at many frequencies, plotted in dB and degrees.

Emphasise
  • Bode = repeated sine tests.
  • Misconception: Bode plots only apply to electronics.

Transition: After the break: feedback and PID.

Session 2.4 · PID and basic control structures Curriculum code 2.4 · 90 min

Slide 38Feedback: the cruise-control story~9 min

Tell the story. Open loop: fixed throttle, a hill comes, the car slows and nobody reacts. Closed loop: the speedometer measures, the ECU compares with the set speed and corrects the throttle. Name the parts: set point, error, controller, actuator, plant, sensor, disturbance. Then the P-only example: plant gain 2, controller 4.5, loop gain 9, speed 9/10 of 100 = 90 km/h. A 10 km/h offset remains because P needs an error to produce throttle.

Emphasise
  • Measure, compare, correct.
  • Misconception: a P controller reaches the set point exactly. It leaves an offset.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why not use a huge K_p? A: Offset shrinks but noise and oscillation grow, as we will see.

Transition: Now the same loop in general notation.

Slide 39The standard feedback loop~7 min

Replace the car by G(s) and the ECU by C(s). The loop gain L = CG is what you get by going once around. S = 1/(1+L) is the part of a disturbance that survives; T = L/(1+L) is how well y follows r. For the cruise control L = 9 gives S = 0.1, T = 0.9, and S + T = 1 always. Show Watt's governor as the first P controller.

Emphasise
  • L, S, T will be used until the end of the day.
  • Misconception: high loop gain has no cost; noise passes through.

Transition: What do P, I and D each contribute?

Slide 40P, I and D: present, past and future~9 min

P reacts to the error now, I to the accumulated past, D to the trend, a guess of the future. Left plot: more P gives faster response but a remaining offset and more overshoot. Right plot: adding I removes the offset; adding D damps the overshoot. Use the table as the summary. Link back to the cruise control: I keeps adding throttle until the offset is zero.

Emphasise
  • I removes the steady-state error.
  • Misconception: D makes the loop faster. It mainly adds damping.

Transition: Different vendors write PID differently.

Slide 41PID forms~5 min

Same controller, different parameters: parallel with K_p, K_i, K_d, or ideal with K_p, T_i, T_d. Do the conversion example: K_p = 2, T_i = 4 s gives K_i = 0.5 per second; T_d = 0.5 s gives K_d = 1 s. Warn about copying gains between devices with different forms or units.

Emphasise
  • Check form and units before entering gains.
  • Misconception: T_i and K_i are the same thing.

Transition: How do we choose the numbers? The classic recipe.

Slide 42Ziegler–Nichols~8 min

Recipe: P only, raise the gain until the loop keeps oscillating; note K_u and T_u; use the table. Work the example step by step: three poles lag 180 degrees at sqrt(3) rad/s, gain there is 1/8, so K_u = 8, T_u = 3.63 s, PID 4.8, 1.81 s, 0.45 s. Warn that the result is aggressive and that pushing a real machine into oscillation can be dangerous.

Emphasise
  • ZN is a starting point only.
  • Misconception: ZN gives the 'optimal' tuning.

Transition: A safer way uses a model.

Slide 43Model-based tuning~7 min

With the FOPDT model from 2.3 choose how fast you want the closed loop, tau_c, and compute K_p. Do the steps: SIMC gives 0.57, lambda gives 0.35, both with T_i = 1.29 s; ZN would give 1.02. The 3:1 spread shows that rules aim at different robustness. Cohen–Coon is Going further.

Emphasise
  • One knob: desired closed-loop time constant.
  • Misconception: there is one correct tuning.

Transition: A closer look at the D-term.

Slide 44Derivative action: always filter it~6 min

A pure derivative amplifies fast wiggles without limit, so sensor noise becomes huge: see the red line rising forever. The filter caps it at K_p N. Taking the derivative of the measurement instead of the error avoids a kick when the set point jumps. In servos the D-part on position is simply velocity feedback. Encoder example: one count gives a speed jump of 0.048 rad/s.

Emphasise
  • Always filter D.
  • Misconception: more D is always more damping. Noise limits it.

Transition: Another practical trick: set-point weighting.

Slide 45Set-point weighting~5 min

Use only part of the set point in the P-term; the I-term keeps the true error, so the final value is still exact. Overshoot drops from 33 to 0.3 percent while the disturbance response is unchanged. Recipe: tune for disturbances first, then pick b. In servos the same idea is a smooth trajectory plus feedforward.

Emphasise
  • Changes command response, not stability.
  • Misconception: b changes the loop gain.

Transition: What happens when the actuator hits its limit?

Slide 46Windup and anti-windup~9 min

Back to the car: going uphill with full throttle, still too slow. The integrator keeps adding error although the throttle cannot open further. At the hill top it must unwind first: 18 percent overshoot and slow settling, red curve. Two cures: clamping stops integrating while saturated; back-calculation bleeds the integrator so it follows the limit. Both curves recover quickly.

Emphasise
  • Every saturating integrator needs anti-windup.
  • Misconception: windup is a bug in the plant. It is in the controller.

Transition: Feedback reacts; feedforward anticipates.

Slide 47Feedforward~8 min

A driver who sees the hill presses the pedal before the car slows: feedforward. For a servo, the needed torque is inertia times acceleration plus friction, computed from the planned trajectory. Error drops from 8.8 to 0.86 mrad even with a 10 percent wrong inertia. Feedforward sits outside the loop, so it does not affect stability. It needs a smooth reference.

Emphasise
  • FF handles the known part, feedback the unknown.
  • Misconception: feedforward can replace feedback.

Transition: Real drives nest several loops.

Slide 48Cascade control~9 min

Use the company analogy: the fast inner loop does what the outer loop asks. Current loop inside speed loop inside position loop, each 4 to 10 times slower. Walk through the table with the motor from 2.2: current PI 15.1 V/A and 2 ms; speed PI 1.26 and 6.4 ms; position P 126 per second. Tune from the inside out. Details on Day 4.

Emphasise
  • Tune inside-out with bandwidth separation.
  • Misconception: all loops can be tuned at once.

Transition: Check your understanding.

Slide 49Session 2.4 – Check your understanding~4 min

Answers: 1) With P only, extra throttle needs an error, so a small speed shortfall remains; the I-term removes it. 2) An unfiltered derivative amplifies high-frequency noise without limit. 3) The integrator winds up to a huge value; at the top the car overshoots the set speed until it unwinds. Anti-windup prevents this.

Emphasise
  • Tie every answer to the cruise control.
  • Misconception: offset means the controller is broken.

Transition: Summary.

Slide 50Session 2.4 – Summary and key terms~4 min

Read the six take-aways and the key terms. Ask one student to explain loop gain and sensitivity using the cruise-control numbers.

Emphasise
  • PID plus filter, anti-windup and feedforward.
  • Misconception: PID tuning is just guessing.

Transition: Last session: is the loop stable, and how robust?

Slide 52What does “stable” mean?~6 min

Ball in a bowl returns, ball on a hill runs away, ball on a flat table stays where pushed. Then the shower: hot water arrives late; if you over-react, the temperature swings more and more. Feedback can create instability. Closed-loop poles are the roots of 1 + L = 0; stable means all in the left half-plane. Example: K = 20 stable, K = 100 unstable.

Emphasise
  • Stability is decided by closed-loop poles.
  • Misconception: feedback always stabilises.

Transition: How do we describe a good response?

Slide 53Time-domain specifications~7 min

Define rise time, overshoot, settling time and steady-state error on the plot. For a second-order loop zeta alone fixes the overshoot; read the table: 0.4 gives 25 percent, 0.7 gives under 5. Rule of thumb: phase margin in degrees is about 100 times zeta, which links to the Bode plot later.

Emphasise
  • zeta fixes overshoot; PM approx 100 zeta.
  • Misconception: settling time and rise time are the same.

Transition: What remains at the end: steady-state error.

Slide 54Steady-state error and system type~7 min

Final value theorem from the Laplace table, only for stable loops. Count integrators in L: type 0 leaves a step offset (cruise control with P), type 1 none for steps but a constant lag for ramps. Do the servo steps: K_v = 126, motor at 126 rad/s, lag 1 rad = 1.6 mm. Velocity feedforward removes it.

Emphasise
  • Integrators in L decide the steady error.
  • Misconception: FVT works for unstable loops.

Slide 55Root locus~7 min

The curves show where the three closed-loop poles go as K increases. Start at the open-loop poles, two meet and split into an oscillating pair, at K = 20 we get 37 percent overshoot, at K = 70 they hit the imaginary axis: the limit. Sketching rules are Going further.

Emphasise
  • More gain moves poles to the right.
  • Misconception: more gain always means better control.

Transition: Two classic tests, only as concepts.

Slide 56Going further: Routh–Hurwitz and Nyquist~4 min

Keep this short. Routh decides stability from the coefficients without solving: here 0 < K < 70, the same as the root locus. Nyquist plots L(j omega) in the complex plane; the loop is stable if the curve does not encircle minus 1. The distance from minus 1 measures robustness, and margins are a simple reading of it.

Emphasise
  • Optional; concepts only.
  • Misconception: these are needed to design a PID. Margins suffice.

Transition: Margins on the Bode plot.

Slide 57Gain and phase margins~8 min

The loop oscillates if a signal returns with gain 1 and minus 180 degrees. Margins are the safety distance, like distance to a cliff edge. Do the three steps on the plot: crossover at 1.52, phase minus 144, PM 36 degrees; phase minus 180 at 3.16, gain minus 10.9 dB, GM 10.9 dB. Targets 45 to 60 degrees and at least 6 dB. A delay of 0.41 s would use up all the phase margin.

Emphasise
  • Read PM at |L| = 0 dB, GM at phase -180.
  • Misconception: a stable loop with PM 5 degrees is fine; it rings badly.

Transition: One more robustness measure: sensitivity.

Slide 58Sensitivity, robustness and loop shaping~7 min

|S| shows how much of a disturbance survives at each frequency. At low frequency it is small; near crossover it rises above 1, so feedback amplifies disturbances there. The peak M_s should be 1.2 to 2. K = 20 has M_s 2.1 and rings; K = 10 is calmer. Loop shaping: big |L| at low frequency, small at high frequency. Formulas and waterbed effect are Going further.

Emphasise
  • Aim for M_s below 2.
  • Misconception: feedback reduces disturbances at all frequencies.

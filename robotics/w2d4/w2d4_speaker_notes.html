Session 5.6 · Material handling and flexible systems Curriculum code 5.6 · 60 min · incl. opening

Slide 2Agenda and learning outcomes~3 min

Welcome to day four of week two. Today has two halves. In the morning we finish the manufacturing topic: how parts move between cells, and how we keep people safe around robots. After lunch we start the sensor block: what makes a good measurement, how a robot knows the angle of its joints, and how it senses force, presence and distance. Read the outcomes on the right aloud: they are the things you should be able to do tonight. Each session ends with a short check and a list of key terms.

Emphasise
  • Today links two worlds: the factory floor (flow, safety) and the sensor that makes both possible.
  • Misconception: safety is an add-on at the end. It is designed in from the start.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why sensors on the same day as safety? A: Every safety function starts with a sensor: a light curtain, a scanner, a door switch.

Transition: Let us first remember where we stopped yesterday.

Slide 3What we learned so far: the robot work-cell (W2D3)~4 min

Yesterday we designed one robot cell. We saw typical applications such as welding and palletising, laid out a cell with reach and cycle time in mind, programmed a PLC sequence, and talked about frames on the teach pendant. Three ideas come back today. Cycle time: the slowest station sets the pace. PLC inputs: every sensor we meet this afternoon ends up as a PLC or controller input. Frames: they describe where things are, and sensors are how we find out. Today we connect cells together and add people to the picture.

Emphasise
  • Cycle time and bottleneck return in the FMS example.
  • Misconception: a cell is designed in isolation. In reality the material flow around it often limits output.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What sets the cycle time of a line? A: The slowest station, the bottleneck.

Transition: So let us look at how material moves through a factory.

Slide 5Why material flow matters~5 min

Start with the airport analogy: your suitcase spends minutes in the plane and hours on belts and trolleys. A traditional job shop is similar. The bar chart is illustrative, but the message is well known in manufacturing: a part spends most of its time waiting in queues, not being machined. Material handling means moving, storing and buffering parts. If we make the robot twice as fast but parts still wait, output barely changes. Flexible handling also lets one line build many variants, which is the point of flexible automation.

Emphasise
  • Speeding up the machine does not help if parts wait.
  • Misconception: automation is mainly about faster machines; much of the gain is in flow.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Where would you look first to raise output? A: At queues and the bottleneck, not at the fastest machine.

Transition: The simplest handling device is the conveyor.

Slide 6Conveyors: the factory's moving pavement~6 min

A conveyor is like the moving walkway at an airport. A motor drives a drum through a gearbox; the belt moves at the drum's surface speed. The equation is just circumference times revolutions per second: v equals pi D times n over i. Walk through the example: 1500 rpm is 25 revolutions per second, the 25 to 1 gear gives one revolution per second at the drum, and a 0.1 metre drum gives about 0.31 metres per second. With parts every half metre, that is roughly 37 parts per minute. Mention accumulating conveyors, which let parts queue without pressure.

Emphasise
  • Units: convert rpm to rev/s before using the formula.
  • Misconception: forgetting to divide by the gear ratio, giving a belt 25 times too fast.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What happens to throughput if parts are spaced at 0.25 m? A: It doubles, to about 75 parts per minute, if the downstream cell can keep up.

Transition: Robots often have to pick parts straight off such a moving belt.

Slide 7Conveyor tracking: picking from a moving belt~6 min

Conveyor tracking means the robot works on a moving belt without stopping it. A camera or photo-sensor detects the part and at that instant latches the conveyor encoder count. From then on the robot controller knows where the part is: the start position plus the counts travelled times the distance per count. When the part enters the pick window, the robot moves with the belt, matching speed, and grips it. In the example, 4000 counts per drum revolution and a 314 millimetre circumference give about 0.08 millimetres per count, far finer than needed.

Emphasise
  • The encoder on the conveyor is the link between belt and robot: we meet encoders properly in 6.2.
  • Misconception: the camera must see the part at the moment of picking. It only needs to see it once.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What if the belt slips on the drum? A: Then counts no longer equal distance; tracking errors appear. Use a measuring wheel on the belt itself.

Transition: Conveyors follow fixed paths. Vehicles give more freedom.

Slide 8AGV and AMR: vehicles that move material~6 min

Compare the two vehicle types. An AGV is like a train on invisible rails: it follows a wire in the floor, magnetic tape, or floor codes, and when something blocks its path it stops and waits. An AMR is more like a taxi: it carries a map of the building, usually built with a LiDAR, localises itself, and plans a path around obstacles. Changing an AGV route means relaying tape; changing an AMR route means editing a map. The photo shows a mobile platform next to an industrial robot. The SLAM remark is optional depth.

Emphasise
  • AGV: fixed guide, predictable. AMR: free navigation, flexible.
  • Misconception: AMRs are always better. For heavy, repetitive routes the simpler AGV is often the right choice.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Which would you choose for a fixed 2-tonne pallet route? A: Usually an AGV: predictable, proven, and the route never changes.

Transition: How many of these vehicles do we actually need?

Slide 9Worked example: how many AMRs do we need?~7 min

Go through the steps slowly. Demand is 60 transports per hour. One round trip is 200 metres loaded and 200 metres empty at one metre per second, so 400 seconds of driving, plus 60 seconds to load and 60 to unload, so 520 seconds, about 8.7 minutes. One AMR therefore makes about 6.9 trips per hour, and we need 60 divided by 6.9, about 8.7, so nine vehicles. But vehicles charge, wait in traffic and break down. With 80 percent availability we need 9 divided by 0.8, about 11.3, so twelve AMRs. Always round up.

Emphasise
  • Round up at each step that represents whole vehicles.
  • Misconception: ignoring loading time and availability gives a fleet that is 30 percent too small.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What if the AMR speed rises to 1.5 m/s? A: Travel 267 s, round trip 387 s, about 9.3 trips/h, so 6.5 → 7 AMRs, 7/0.8 ≈ 8.8 → 9 AMRs.

Transition: Now put machines and transport together into one flexible system.

Slide 10Flexible manufacturing system (FMS) cell~6 min

A flexible manufacturing system combines several CNC machines, an automated transport system and a cell computer that decides which pallet goes where. Parts are clamped on pallets with fixtures, so any machine can take any pallet, and robots load and unload. Point at the drawing: machines on top, transport in the middle, load station and pallet store below. Then the bottleneck example: stations of 40, 60 and 45 seconds. The line can only go as fast as the 60-second station, so 60 parts per hour, and the other stations wait part of the time.

Emphasise
  • The bottleneck sets the output; improve it first.
  • Misconception: average station time sets the pace. It is the maximum.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: How could we raise output to 90 parts per hour? A: Duplicate the 60 s station or split its work so no station exceeds 40 s.

Transition: Some of these cells now share space with people, which brings us to cobots.

Slide 11Collaborative robots (cobots)~5 min

A cobot is designed to share workspace with people without a fence, if the risk assessment allows it. Cobots are light, rounded, avoid pinch points, and sense joint torque or motor current so they can detect a collision and stop. They are also easy to teach by hand. The photo shows a typical six-axis cobot on a workbench. Stress the note at the bottom: a cobot holding a knife is not safe. Safety belongs to the whole application, including the tool, the part, and the speed, not to the robot alone.

Emphasise
  • Collaborative is a property of the application, not of the robot.
  • Misconception: buying a cobot removes the need for a risk assessment.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Is a cobot slower than an industrial robot? A: Usually yes in collaborative mode, because speed and force are limited; it can run faster when no person is near.

Transition: The standards describe four ways in which people and robots can collaborate.

Slide 12Human–robot collaboration: four modes~6 min

Walk through the four panels from left to right. Safety-rated monitored stop: the robot stops, under safety monitoring, while the person is in the shared space, and resumes when they leave. Hand guiding: the person moves the robot using a handle with an enabling switch. Speed and separation monitoring: the robot slows as the person comes closer and stops before contact. Power and force limiting: contact may happen, but force and pressure stay below limits for each body region, given in ISO/TS 15066. Real cells usually combine modes. The energy formula is optional depth.

Emphasise
  • Only power and force limiting allows contact during motion.
  • Misconception: the four modes are robot types. They are ways of operating.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Which mode suits a heavy payload near people? A: Speed and separation monitoring, since contact with a heavy payload could not stay under force limits.

Transition: Let us check what you remember from this session.

Slide 13Check your understanding: material handling~4 min

Give students two minutes in pairs, then take answers. Answers: one, v equals pi times 0.2 times 0.5, about 0.31 metres per second. Two, an AGV stops and waits, usually raising an alarm, until someone removes the box, because it can only follow its guide; an AMR plans a path around the box if there is space. Three, no: the cell needs a risk assessment, and a sharp tool makes contact unacceptable, so we need speed and separation monitoring, guarding or a tool cover, not just a cobot.

Emphasise
  • Listen for the word application in question three.
  • Misconception: the cobot alone makes the cell safe.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Q1 A: 0.31 m/s
  • Q: Q2 A: AGV stops and waits; AMR replans around
  • Q: Q3 A: No: the application, including the tool, must be assessed

Transition: Let us summarise and collect the key terms.

Slide 14Summary and key terms: material handling~2 min

Summarise in one breath: parts mostly wait, so flow matters. Conveyor speed is pi D n over i, and tracking uses an encoder on the belt. AGVs follow fixed guides, AMRs navigate with maps, and fleet size comes from demand times round trip divided by availability. An FMS combines machines, transport and a scheduling computer, and its output is set by the bottleneck. Cobots support four collaborative modes, but each application must be assessed. Point to the key terms box; students should be able to define each.

Emphasise

  • Misconception: collaborative equals safe.

Session 5.7 · Safety and standards Curriculum code 5.7 · 60 min

Slide 16Why robots are dangerous~5 min

Start from physics students know: kinetic energy is one half m v squared. A robot arm with an effective moving mass of 20 kilograms at 2 metres per second carries 40 joules, about the same as a 4 kilogram brick falling one metre. The plot shows that energy grows with the square of speed, so halving speed quarters the energy. Hazards are impact, crushing and trapping between robot and fixed objects, thrown parts and the tool itself. Robots also move fast, without warning, and may start when someone thinks they are stopped.

Emphasise
  • Speed counts twice: energy goes with v squared.
  • Misconception: a robot standing still is safe. It may be waiting for a signal.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Which reduces energy more: halving mass or halving speed? A: Halving speed: energy drops to one quarter instead of one half.

Transition: So how do we deal with these hazards systematically? With ISO 12100.

Slide 17ISO 12100: the risk assessment loop~7 min

ISO 12100 is the basic standard for all machinery. It defines risk as the combination of how severe a harm could be and how likely it is. The process is a loop. First, determine the limits of the machine: what it does, who uses it, where. Second, identify hazards. Third, estimate the risk. Fourth, evaluate: is it acceptable? If not, reduce the risk and go back, because every new safeguard can create new hazards. The first three steps together are called risk analysis. Everything is documented.

Emphasise
  • It is a loop, not a checklist done once.
  • Misconception: risk equals the probability of an accident. It is severity and probability together.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Example of a safeguard that creates a new hazard? A: A fence creates a trapping zone between fence and robot if someone can be locked inside.

Transition: Step five, risk reduction, follows a strict order of preference.

Slide 18Three-step risk reduction and the standards family~6 min

The pyramid shows the three-step method. Step one, the most effective: design the hazard away, for example lower speed, lower mass, or no pinch points. Step two: add safeguards such as fences, interlocked doors and light curtains. Step three, the weakest: information for use, meaning warnings, signs, training and protective equipment. On the right is the family of standards. Type A, ISO 12100, applies to everything. Type B covers generic aspects like safety control systems and safety distances. Type C covers one machine type: ISO 10218 for industrial robots, plus ISO/TS 15066 for collaboration.

Emphasise
  • Design first, guard second, warn last.
  • Misconception: a warning sign is an acceptable fix for a high risk.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Where does a light curtain belong in the pyramid? A: Step two, safeguarding.

Transition: How strong must a safeguard's control system be? The risk graph answers that.

Slide 19How strong must a safety function be? The risk graph~8 min

ISO 13849-1 asks three questions. S: how severe is the injury, slight or serious? F: how often and how long is someone exposed, rarely or frequently? P: can the person avoid the hazard, possible or hardly? Follow the tree from start: each path ends in a required performance level from a, the weakest, to e, the strongest. Work through the example: an operator reaches into the cell several times per hour, injury could be serious and the robot is too fast to dodge, so S2, F2, P2, giving PL e. Robot safety functions are typically required at PL d.

Emphasise
  • PLr comes from the risk; PL comes from the design. The design must reach PLr.
  • Misconception: higher PL means safer machine overall. It only describes one safety function's reliability.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What if the operator enters only once per shift? A: F1: S2-F1-P2 gives PL d.

Transition: How do we build a control system that reaches PL d or e?

Slide 20Performance level and redundant (two-channel) design~7 min

The table gives the meaning of each PL as the average probability of a dangerous failure per hour. PL d means at most one in a million per hour: around one dangerous failure in more than a century of continuous operation. No single relay or sensor is that reliable on its own, so we use redundancy. The drawing shows category 3: two sensor channels, two logic units that cross-check each other, and two contactors with feedback so a welded contact is detected. One fault does not remove the safety function, and the fault is detected.

Emphasise
  • Redundancy plus diagnostics, not just better parts.
  • Misconception: two identical channels are enough. Without cross-checking, a hidden fault in one channel stays undetected until the second fails.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why the feedback from the contactors? A: A contactor can weld shut; feedback lets the logic notice and block a restart.

Transition: Now a concrete safeguard: the light curtain, and how far away it must be.

Slide 21Light curtains and the safety distance~8 min

A light curtain is a row of infrared beams; blocking any beam sends a stop signal. But the robot does not stop instantly, and a hand keeps moving. ISO 13855 gives the minimum distance S equals K times T plus C. K is approach speed, 2000 millimetres per second for a hand. T is the total stopping time of curtain plus robot. C is an allowance for reaching through before detection, 8 times d minus 14 for detection capability d. Example: T is 0.2 seconds, d is 14 millimetres so C is zero, and S is 400 millimetres.

Emphasise
  • Stopping time must be measured on the real robot, with payload.
  • Misconception: the curtain can be placed anywhere because it reacts in milliseconds; the robot's stop takes much longer.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What if the robot needs 0.4 s to stop? A: S = 2000·0.42 = 840 mm; and for distances over 500 mm the standard allows K = 1600 mm/s, worth checking.

Transition: Light curtains guard a line. Laser scanners guard an area.

Slide 22Laser scanners and speed & separation monitoring~5 min

A safety laser scanner sweeps a light beam in a fan and measures distance by time of flight, which we explain in session 6.3. You configure fields on the floor. If someone enters the outer warning field, the robot slows down. If they enter the inner protective field, it stops. This is the basis of speed and separation monitoring: the allowed speed depends on the separation distance. The analogy is adaptive cruise control in a car: the closer the car ahead, the slower you go, and you must always be able to stop in time.

Emphasise
  • Warning field slows, protective field stops.
  • Misconception: a scanner can be configured however you like. Field sizes come from the same stopping-distance calculation as light curtains.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why not just stop at the outer field? A: Productivity: slowing allows work to continue while the person is still far away.

Transition: Finally, what does stopping actually mean, and who decides?

Slide 23Emergency stop, stop categories and the safety PLC~6 min

IEC 60204-1 defines three stop categories. In category 0 the power is cut at once and the axis coasts or is held by a brake. In category 1 the drive brakes the axis under control, then power is cut. In category 2 the axis is braked but power stays on, for example for a monitored standstill. The plot compares them. The emergency stop button is red on yellow, latching and wired with two channels. A safety PLC has redundant processors and self-tests. Safe torque off means the drive removes torque without opening the motor cable.

Emphasise
  • Category 1 often stops faster and more gently than category 0 for a heavy axis.
  • Misconception: an emergency stop is a normal stop button. It is a complementary measure, not the main safeguard.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why can category 0 be dangerous on a vertical axis? A: Without power the axis can fall; it needs a holding brake.

Transition: Time for a check on safety.

Slide 24Check your understanding: safety~5 min

Let students work individually for two minutes. Answers: one, inherently safe design first, then safeguarding and complementary protective measures, then information for use. Two, S equals 2000 times 0.15 plus 8 times 14 minus 14, which is 300 plus zero, so 300 millimetres. Three, any single component can fail in a dangerous way, for example a welded contact or a stuck input; with two channels that monitor each other, one failure is detected and the other channel still stops the machine. No single part is reliable enough for PL d.

Emphasise
  • Order of the three steps matters.
  • Misconception: a very reliable single channel is equivalent to redundancy.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Q1 A: Safe design → safeguards → information
  • Q: Q2 A: 300 mm
  • Q: Q3 A: Single faults must not remove the safety function and must be detected

Transition: Let us summarise.

Slide 25Summary and key terms: safety and standards~3 min

ISO 12100 gives the loop: limits, hazards, estimate, evaluate, reduce, and repeat. Risk reduction prefers safe design, then safeguards, then information. The risk graph of ISO 13849-1 gives the required performance level, and redundancy with diagnostics reaches it. Safeguards must be placed at a safety distance S equals K T plus C, so the stopping time matters. ISO 10218 and ISO/TS 15066 are the robot-specific standards. Point out that every safety device in this session is a sensor, which leads straight to the afternoon.

Emphasise
  • Every safety function begins with a sensor.
  • Misconception: standards are paperwork. They encode decades of accident experience.

Transition: After the break we begin the sensor block with measurement principles.

Session 6.1 · Sensors and measurement principles Curriculum code 6.1 · 60 min

Slide 27The senses of a robot cell~5 min

Ask students to close their eyes and touch their nose. They can, because their body senses joint positions: that is proprioception. Seeing the cup they reach for is exteroception. A robot cell has both. Proprioceptive sensors measure the robot itself: encoders in every joint, motor currents. Exteroceptive sensors measure the world: the force/torque sensor at the wrist, a camera above the conveyor, proximity sensors detecting parts, light curtains guarding people. A real cell easily has dozens of sensors, each an input to a robot controller or a PLC.

Emphasise
  • Every sensor answers a question the controller cannot answer otherwise.
  • Misconception: the robot knows where the part is because it was programmed. It only knows where it expects the part.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Is a motor current sensor proprio- or exteroceptive? A: Proprioceptive, but it can indirectly reveal external contact.

Transition: Every one of these sensors follows the same chain from physics to numbers.

Slide 28The measurement chain~7 min

Follow the chain from left to right. The measurand is the physical quantity we want, such as a force. The sensor or transducer converts it into something electrical, maybe a tiny resistance change. Signal conditioning amplifies, filters and isolates it into a clean voltage, perhaps zero to ten volts. An ADC or a counter turns it into bits. Processing scales the bits into engineering units like newtons or radians and filters them. Finally the controller uses the value. The red arrows remind us that every stage adds error, noise and delay.

Emphasise
  • A chain is only as good as its weakest link.
  • Misconception: a high-quality sensor guarantees a good measurement; wiring, grounding and the ADC matter just as much.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Where does a long unshielded cable hurt most? A: Between sensor and conditioning, where the signal is still millivolts.

Transition: Let us describe the sensor itself with a few numbers.

Slide 29Static characteristic: sensitivity, offset, range, linearity~9 min

Plot output against input for slow, steady inputs: that is the static characteristic. Ideally it is a straight line, y equals y zero plus S x. The slope S is the sensitivity, here 0.2 volts per newton. The value at zero input is the offset, here 0.4 volts. The range is the span of inputs the sensor is rated for, and full scale is the largest value. Real sensors bend away from the line; the largest gap, expressed as a percentage of full-scale output, is the linearity error. In the example, 0.12 volts out of a 2 volt span is 6 percent.

Emphasise
  • Specs are often in percent of full scale, so small readings have larger relative error.
  • Misconception: linearity error is a percentage of the reading. Usually it is of full scale.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: A 1 % FS error on a 100 N sensor, reading 5 N: relative error? A: 1 N out of 5 N, that is 20 %.

Transition: Now three words students often mix up.

Slide 30Accuracy, precision and resolution~7 min

Use the dartboard. Accuracy is how close the darts land to the bull's eye on average. Precision, or repeatability, is how tightly they cluster. The top-right board is precise but not accurate: a steady bias. Resolution is the smallest change the sensor can show, like the finest marks on a ruler. A ruler with 0.1 millimetre marks printed 2 millimetres off is high resolution but inaccurate. Robots show this beautifully: an industrial robot repeats a taught point very precisely, about five hundredths of a millimetre, but reaches a computed point only to a few tenths.

Emphasise
  • This is why we teach points by hand rather than typing coordinates.
  • Misconception: more digits on the display means more accuracy.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Which matters for a robot replaying taught points? A: Repeatability (precision).

Transition: Errors come in two kinds, and each has its own cure.

Slide 31Random and systematic errors~9 min

Measure the same 10 kilogram mass many times and draw a histogram: a bell curve. Its centre is the mean; the gap from the true value is the systematic error, or bias. Its width, the standard deviation sigma, is the random error. Bias is fixed by calibration; random error is reduced by averaging. Work the example: five readings sum to 50.5, so the mean is 10.1, a bias of plus 0.1. Deviations are 0.1, minus 0.1, 0, minus 0.2, 0.2; squares sum to 0.10; divide by n minus 1, which is 4, and take the root: about 0.16 kilograms.

Emphasise
  • Averaging n readings shrinks random error by about the square root of n, but never removes bias.
  • Misconception: averaging fixes every error.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why n−1 and not n? A: Because the mean was estimated from the same data; dividing by n−1 corrects the slight underestimate. Not essential today.

Transition: So how do we remove the bias? By calibration.

Slide 32Calibration: making the numbers right~8 min

Calibration means applying known reference inputs and recording the outputs, then using the fitted line to convert future readings. With a two-point calibration we use two references. Steps: zero kilograms gives 0.40 volts, ten kilograms gives 2.40 volts. Sensitivity is the rise over run: 2 volts over 10 kilograms, 0.2 volts per kilogram. Offset is 0.40 volts. Now invert: a reading of 1.30 volts means 1.30 minus 0.40, divided by 0.20, which is 4.5 kilograms. Mention that in robotics calibration also means finding true link lengths, camera parameters or the tool frame.

Emphasise
  • Calibrate with references at least a few times more accurate than the sensor.
  • Misconception: calibration improves precision. It removes bias; noise remains.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why two points rather than one? A: One point fixes only the offset; two fix offset and sensitivity.

Transition: So far the input changed slowly. What if it jumps?

Slide 33Dynamic behaviour: sensors are not instant~7 min

Put a kitchen thermometer into hot soup: the reading climbs and settles. Many sensors behave like the first-order system from W1D2: y of t equals the final value times one minus e to the minus t over tau. After one time constant the reading has reached 63 percent; after three, 95 percent. Example: a sensor with tau of 2 milliseconds needs 6 milliseconds to reach 95 percent, so a 1 kilohertz control loop sees the change only after six cycles. That delay eats phase margin, as we learned in the control block.

Emphasise
  • Sensor delay is part of the loop delay.
  • Misconception: digital sensors are instantaneous. They also filter and need conversion time.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: How long for 99 %? A: About 4.6 τ, roughly 5 τ.

Transition: Let us check measurement principles.

Slide 34Check your understanding: measurement principles~5 min

Give two minutes. Answers: one, the readings are tightly grouped but about 0.23 kilograms too high, so precise but not accurate; calibration would fix it. Two, sensitivity is 2 volts over 100 newtons, 0.02 volts per newton; 2.2 minus 1.0 is 1.2 volts, divided by 0.02 gives 60 newtons. Three, 95 percent takes three time constants, 3 times 4 seconds, 12 seconds. If students say both accurate and precise for question one, return to the dartboard.

Emphasise
  • Precise but biased is the most common real-world case.
  • Misconception: close to the true value means accurate even if consistently off.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Q1 A: Precise, not accurate
  • Q: Q2 A: 60 N
  • Q: Q3 A: 12 s

Transition: Summary and key terms.

Slide 35Summary and key terms: measurement principles~3 min

Recap the chain: sensor, conditioning, ADC, processing. Static description: sensitivity, offset, range and linearity. Keep accuracy, precision and resolution apart. Fix bias by calibration and reduce noise by averaging. Dynamics: a time constant tau, with 95 percent after three tau. Ask students to define two key terms to a neighbour. These ideas are the vocabulary for every sensor we meet next.

Emphasise
  • These terms apply to every sensor in 6.2 and 6.3.
  • Misconception: resolution and accuracy are the same.

Transition: Now the most important sensor in any robot: the joint position sensor.

Session 6.2 · Position and motion sensors Curriculum code 6.2 · 60 min

Slide 37Where position sensors sit in a robot joint~5 min

Every robot joint has a position sensor on the motor shaft, behind a gearbox. That placement is clever: the gear multiplies resolution. A 17-bit motor encoder has 131,072 counts per revolution; behind a 100 to 1 gear that becomes 13 million counts per joint revolution, about thirty millionths of a degree. But the motor-side sensor cannot see backlash or twist in the gear, which we met in W1D2. High-accuracy robots therefore add a secondary encoder on the load side. Today: encoders, resolvers and inertial sensors.

Emphasise
  • Motor-side sensing is fine-grained but blind to gear errors.
  • Misconception: high counts per revolution means the tool position is equally precise.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why not put only one encoder on the load side? A: It would see the gear's elasticity inside the loop, making control harder; it is also more expensive.

Transition: Before encoders, a quick refresher on binary numbers.

Slide 38Refresher: binary numbers and Gray code~5 min

Each binary digit is a power of two. 101 in binary is four plus zero plus one, five. N bits give 2 to the N values. Now look at going from 3 to 4: 011 becomes 100, all three bits flip. On a real disk the three edges never line up perfectly, so for a moment the reader could see 111 or 000, a wild jump. Gray code changes only one bit per step, so a reader on the boundary sees either the old or the new value, never nonsense. The disk drawing shows three Gray-coded tracks.

Emphasise
  • Gray code avoids glitches at boundaries.
  • Misconception: Gray code gives more resolution. It gives the same resolution, but safer reading.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: How many bits for 0.01° resolution? A: 36,000 positions → 16 bits (65,536).

Transition: With that, let us look at the most common sensor: the incremental encoder.

Slide 39Optical incremental encoders~6 min

An incremental encoder has a disk with N slits. An LED shines through onto photodiodes, which produce square waves called A and B, shifted by a quarter period, plus one index pulse Z per revolution. The drive counts pulses, so it only knows how far it moved since switch-on: position is relative and a homing run is needed. The photo shows such a disk. Example: 2500 lines at 6000 rpm, 100 revolutions per second, gives 250 kilohertz on channel A, so the counter input must handle at least that frequency.

Emphasise
  • Incremental means relative: homing after power-up.
  • Misconception: the Z pulse gives absolute position. It only marks one point per revolution.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What happens if the counter misses pulses at high speed? A: Position drifts silently; drives monitor for this with frequency limits and error flags.

Transition: Why two channels A and B? Because of direction and resolution.

Slide 40Quadrature signals and ×4 counting~6 min

A and B are in quadrature, a quarter period apart. If we count every rising and falling edge of both signals we get four counts per line: 4N counts per revolution. Direction comes from order: if A changes before B, we turn forward; otherwise backward. If both change at once, a count was missed and the decoder raises an error. Example: 2500 lines give 10,000 counts per revolution, so 0.036 degrees per count.

Emphasise
  • Four times the lines, for free, in the decoder.
  • Misconception: a single channel is enough. It gives no direction and only N or 2N counts.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Where have we seen quadrature before? A: Sine and cosine 90° apart in FOC (W1D5) — the same idea.

Transition: Incremental encoders forget everything at power-off. Absolute encoders do not.

Slide 41Absolute encoders: no homing after power-up~5 min

An absolute encoder has many Gray-coded tracks, so every angle has its own code and the position is known at switch-on. Single-turn encoders give 17 to 23 bits per revolution. Multi-turn encoders also count whole revolutions, with gears or a battery-backed counter. Industrial robots use multi-turn absolute encoders, so after a power cut the arm knows its pose immediately. The position is sent as a digital message over a serial line: SSI is simple; BiSS-C and EnDat add two-way communication and a CRC check.

Emphasise
  • Absolute equals known position at power-up.
  • Misconception: absolute encoders need no reference ever. After a motor change, the zero must still be set once.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why does the robot sometimes need a battery change? A: The battery keeps the multi-turn counter alive while power is off.

Transition: A completely different principle survives heat and vibration: the resolver.

Slide 42Resolver: a rotating transformer~6 min

A resolver is a rotating transformer with no electronics inside the motor. The rotor coil is fed with a sine carrier, a few kilohertz. Two stator coils at ninety degrees pick up the carrier multiplied by sine theta and by cosine theta. The top trace is the carrier; the next two show the received signals, whose envelopes, dashed, are the sine and cosine of the angle. The electronics recover the angle with atan2 of the two envelopes. Resolvers are robust to heat, shock and vibration, so they are common in harsh environments.

Emphasise
  • The angle lives in the envelopes, not in the carrier.
  • Misconception: a resolver outputs a digital angle. It outputs analogue signals; a converter computes the angle.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why atan2 rather than arcsin? A: atan2 uses both signals, covers 360° and is insensitive to amplitude changes.

Transition: Robots also need speed, and that is harder at low speed than you might think.

Slide 43Speed from an encoder and the low-speed problem~6 min

Speed is derived from position. The M-method counts pulses in a fixed window; the T-method times one pulse period. Work the example: 10,000 counts per revolution and a 125 microsecond sample. One count per sample corresponds to 0.8 revolutions per second, 48 rpm. That is the smallest speed step the M-method can see. At 60 rpm the reading jumps between 48 and 96 rpm, which makes the velocity loop noisy. Remedies are more counts per revolution, longer windows, or filtering, which we discuss tomorrow in 6.6.

Emphasise
  • Low speed is the hard case for M-method speed measurement.
  • Misconception: a fine position sensor automatically gives a smooth speed signal.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: At 6000 rpm, how many counts per sample? A: 100 rev/s × 10,000 × 125 µs = 125 counts: plenty.

Transition: Encoders measure rotation relative to the base. Inertial sensors measure motion relative to space.

Slide 44Accelerometers: a mass on a spring~7 min

An accelerometer is a mass on a spring inside a case. When the case accelerates, the mass lags and the spring deflects by x equals m over k times a. In a MEMS chip like the one in the photo, the mass is a tiny silicon structure and a capacitor measures the gap. At rest the accelerometer measures gravity, so it tells us tilt: the component along x is g times sine theta. Example: 4.9 metres per second squared at rest means sine theta is 0.5, so the sensor is tilted 30 degrees.

Emphasise
  • At rest an accelerometer reads gravity, not zero.
  • Misconception: an accelerometer reading zero means the object is at rest. In free fall it also reads zero.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Use in robotics? A: Tilt of an AMR or a mobile manipulator, vibration measurement on the arm, collision detection.

Transition: Rotation rate needs a different sensor: the gyroscope.

Slide 45Gyroscopes and the IMU: drift and fusion~6 min

A gyroscope measures angular rate in degrees per second; MEMS gyros use a vibrating mass and the Coriolis force. To get angle we integrate the rate, and any small bias integrates too. The orange line shows a bias of just 0.01 degrees per second: after ten minutes, 600 seconds, the angle is 6 degrees off. An IMU combines three accelerometers and three gyros. The trick is fusion: trust the gyro for fast changes and the accelerometer for the long-term tilt, which gives the bounded blue curve. We see the filters behind this tomorrow.

Emphasise
  • Integration turns a tiny bias into a growing error.
  • Misconception: a gyro measures angle directly. It measures rate.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why not use only the accelerometer for tilt? A: It is disturbed by the robot's own accelerations; it is only reliable on average.

Transition: Let us check position and motion sensors.

Slide 46Check your understanding: position and motion sensors~5 min

Give two minutes. Answers: one, 1000 lines times four is 4000 counts per revolution, and 360 divided by 4000 is 0.09 degrees per count. Two, the incremental encoder needs homing, because it only counts changes since power-up; the absolute encoder reads a unique code for every angle. Three, the full gravity value on the x axis means sine theta equals one: the sensor is tilted 90 degrees, with its x axis pointing along gravity.

Emphasise
  • Relative versus absolute is the key distinction.
  • Misconception: the Z pulse makes an incremental encoder absolute.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Q1 A: 4000 counts/rev, 0.09°/count
  • Q: Q2 A: Incremental: it only counts changes
  • Q: Q3 A: 90° tilt, x axis along gravity

Transition: Summary.

Slide 47Summary and key terms: position and motion sensors~3 min

Recap: each joint has a motor-side encoder and the gear multiplies its resolution. Incremental encoders give A, B and Z with 4N counts per revolution, and need homing. Absolute encoders use Gray code and multi-turn counters, so the pose is known at power-up. Resolvers are robust analogue sensors read with atan2. IMUs combine accelerometers, which see tilt, with gyros, which see rate but drift. Ask students which sensor they would put in a welding robot's joint and why.

Emphasise
  • Know which sensor answers which question.
  • Misconception: one sensor type fits all.

Transition: The last session moves from the robot's own motion to its contact with the world.

Session 6.3 · Force/torque, tactile, proximity and range sensors Curriculum code 6.3 · 60 min · incl. day close

Slide 49A map of robot sensors by what they detect~4 min

This map orders sensors by the distance at which they work. At contact we have force/torque and tactile sensors. From millimetres to centimetres, inductive and capacitive proximity sensors. Photoelectric sensors cover centimetres up to tens of metres. Ultrasonic sensors work from centimetres to several metres. LiDAR and laser scanners reach many metres. Point out the three families: contact, proximity which gives yes or no, and range which gives a distance. Many of these simply switch a PLC input, which links back to yesterday's PLC session.

Emphasise
  • Proximity answers is something there; range answers how far.
  • Misconception: every sensor gives a number; many give only a switching signal.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Which of these could detect a glass bottle? A: Ultrasonic, capacitive, or a retro-reflective photoelectric sensor with a polarising filter.

Transition: We start at contact: measuring force with strain gauges.

Slide 50Strain gauges and the Wheatstone bridge~6 min

A strain gauge is a zig-zag foil resistor glued onto metal. When the metal stretches, the foil gets longer and thinner and its resistance rises: delta R over R equals gauge factor times strain, with a gauge factor about 2. These changes are tiny, so we use a Wheatstone bridge, which outputs only the difference. Example: strain of one in a thousand, 5 volts excitation, four active gauges: the output is about 2 times 10 to the minus 3 times 5, which is 10 millivolts. A load cell is a metal body with such a bridge, rated in millivolts per volt.

Emphasise
  • Millivolt signals need careful amplification and shielding.
  • Misconception: strain is large. Typical strains are below 0.1 %.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why does the bridge help with temperature? A: All gauges warm up equally, so their changes cancel in the bridge difference.

Transition: Put several bridges in one body and you get a six-axis force/torque sensor.

Slide 51Six-axis force/torque sensor at the robot wrist~7 min

A six-axis force/torque sensor sits between the robot flange and the tool. Inside, strain gauges on a stiff metal cross measure three forces and three torques. Uses: force-controlled grinding and polishing, inserting a peg into a hole, detecting collisions and hand-guiding the robot. But the sensor also feels the tool's own weight. Example: a 2 kilogram gripper weighs about 19.6 newtons; with its centre of mass 0.1 metres to the side it adds a torque of about 1.96 newton metres. The controller subtracts these, using the current wrist orientation: gravity compensation.

Emphasise
  • The tool's load direction changes with orientation.
  • Misconception: the F/T sensor measures only contact forces. It measures everything acting on it.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What else must be subtracted at high speed? A: Inertial forces of the tool when it accelerates.

Transition: Force sensors measure total force. Tactile sensors show where it acts.

Slide 52Tactile sensors: a skin for the gripper~4 min

A tactile sensor is like the nerve endings in your fingertip: an array of small pressure cells called taxels. The map shows a ten by ten array with the pressure of a grasped object. Principles: resistive, where pressure lowers resistance; capacitive, where pressure narrows the gap and raises capacitance, as the formula shows; and piezoelectric, which reacts to changes and can sense slip. Tactile sensing tells the gripper where and how hard the object touches, so it can centre a grasp or catch slipping.

Emphasise
  • Location and distribution of force, not just total force.
  • Misconception: tactile sensors replace F/T sensors. They complement them.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Where would you use one? A: Handling fragile or soft objects, e.g. fruit, where grip force must be just enough.

Transition: Now sensors that detect objects without touching them.

Slide 53Inductive and capacitive proximity sensors~5 min

The photo shows a typical cylindrical inductive proximity switch. Inside, a coil and oscillator produce an alternating magnetic field. A metal target nearby absorbs energy through eddy currents, the oscillation drops, and the output switches. It only sees metal. A capacitive sensor uses an electric field and reacts to almost any material, including plastics and liquids. The rated sensing distance is a few millimetres for steel; for other metals it shrinks, as the table shows: about 0.7 for stainless steel and 0.3 to 0.4 for aluminium and copper.

Emphasise
  • Inductive: metal only, very robust against dirt and oil.
  • Misconception: the rated distance applies to every metal.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why are inductive sensors so popular in fixtures? A: Cheap, sealed, no moving parts, immune to coolant and dust.

Transition: For longer distances we use light.

Slide 54Photoelectric sensors: three ways to use a light beam~5 min

Three arrangements. Through-beam: emitter and receiver face each other; an object blocks the beam. Longest range, most reliable, but two units to wire and align. Retro-reflective: one unit sends light to a reflector and back; an object breaks the beam. Shiny objects can fool it by reflecting light themselves. Diffuse: the sensor sees light scattered back by the object itself. Easiest to install, but short range, and dark or distant objects return too little light. Light curtains in session 5.7 are many through-beams in a row.

Emphasise
  • Blocked beam versus returned light.
  • Misconception: diffuse sensors work equally well on all colours.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Best choice to count black boxes on a conveyor? A: Through-beam or retro-reflective: they do not rely on the box reflecting light.

Transition: To measure a distance rather than presence, we time an echo.

Slide 55Ultrasonic range sensors: listening for an echo~5 min

An ultrasonic sensor works like a bat. It sends a short burst of sound and listens for the echo. Distance is speed of sound times echo time divided by two, because the sound travels there and back. Example: an echo after 5.83 milliseconds means 343 times 0.00583 divided by 2, about one metre. But the speed of sound depends on air temperature: at 40 degrees it is about 355 metres per second, so the same echo means 1.035 metres, a 3.5 percent error unless corrected. Ultrasonic sensors work on glass and liquids but have a wide cone.

Emphasise
  • Divide by two for the round trip.
  • Misconception: the speed of sound is constant. It changes about 0.6 m/s per kelvin.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why is ultrasonic good for fill levels? A: It works on liquids and granules regardless of colour or transparency.

Transition: Replace sound with light and we get LiDAR.

Slide 56LiDAR and laser scanners: range with light~5 min

LiDAR uses the same time-of-flight equation, but with the speed of light, 3 times 10 to the 8 metres per second. The photo shows a rotating 3-D LiDAR. A rotating mirror sweeps the beam, producing a point cloud; the small drawing shows a 2-D scan. Safety laser scanners from session 5.7 work this way, and AMRs use LiDAR for mapping. Example: an object one metre away returns light after 6.7 nanoseconds; resolving one centimetre means timing about 67 picoseconds. That is why LiDAR electronics are demanding, while ultrasound is easy to time.

Emphasise
  • Same formula, a million times faster signal.
  • Misconception: LiDAR sees everything. Glass, black or mirror-like surfaces can return too little light.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why does an AMR need LiDAR instead of ultrasound? A: Narrow beam, high angular resolution and range give an accurate map.

Transition: With all these options, how do we choose?

Slide 57Choosing a sensor for the task~4 min

Go through the table as a quick quiz: cover the middle column and ask students to choose before revealing. Steel part in a fixture: inductive. Plastic bottle: photoelectric. Fill level: ultrasonic. Peg insertion with 5 newtons: wrist F/T sensor. Slip: tactile. Person approaching: certified safety scanner or light curtain. AMR navigation: LiDAR. Then the four questions in the box: what material, how far, yes or no or a value, and is it safety-related. Safety functions require certified safety sensors, not ordinary ones.

Emphasise
  • The safety question comes first: it changes the whole selection.
  • Misconception: a standard photoelectric sensor can be used as a safety device.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Can a normal inductive sensor monitor a safety door? A: Not on its own; use a certified safety switch or a safety-rated sensor with diagnostics.

Transition: Check your understanding.

Slide 58Check your understanding: force, proximity and range~4 min

Give two minutes. Answers: one, aluminium has a reduction factor of about 0.3 to 0.4, so roughly 2.5 to 3 millimetres. Two, 343 times 0.0029 divided by 2 is about 0.50 metres. Three, the tool's weight and its torque appear in the sensor reading and change as the wrist turns; without subtracting them, the controller would mistake the tool's own weight for a contact, or miss a real collision hidden in that offset.

Session 2.6 · Filters and compensation Curriculum code 2.6 · 60 min (incl. 7 min opening/recap) · slide times sum to 60 min

Slide 1Title~1 min

Welcome to Day 3. Yesterday you learned feedback, Bode plots, PID and stability margins. Today we use those tools in two directions: better controllers (filters, state space, adaptive and robust ideas, digital control) and the real hardware of a servo drive, from the mains plug to the motor.

Emphasise
  • Today links control theory to real drive hardware.
  • Misconception to watch: 'control is just software' — the hardware sets the limits.

Transition: Let us see the plan for the day.

Slide 2Agenda and learning objectives~2 min

Six blocks: four one-hour control sessions and two half-hour hardware sessions. Read the objectives aloud; they are what students should be able to do tonight. Stress the verbs: explain, calculate, sketch, draw. Each session ends with a summary, key terms and a short check-your-understanding slide done in pairs.

Emphasise
  • Breaks are extra to the 5 h.
  • Every session ends with pair discussion questions.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Do we need to memorise formulas? A: No, the boxed equations and what each symbol means.

Transition: First a quick recap of Day 2.

Slide 3What we learned so far (Day 2 in one slide)~4 min

Recap with the cruise-control analogy: the car measures its speed, compares it to the set speed and opens the throttle more or less. PID: P reacts to the error now, I to the error accumulated, D to how fast the error changes. The Bode plot shows loop gain and phase versus frequency; crossover is where the loop gain is one; the phase margin is how far we are from -180 degrees there. Today's thread: everything either costs phase or buys it back.

Emphasise
  • Phase margin = safety distance from instability.
  • Misconception: 'more gain is always better' — more gain raises crossover and eats margin.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Ask: 'What happens to a loop with zero phase margin?' (It oscillates forever.)

Transition: Let us start with filters.

Slide 5Refresher: what does a filter do?~5 min

Use the music equaliser. Turning down the treble is a low-pass filter; turning down the bass is a high-pass; removing a 50 Hz hum is a notch. The simplest low-pass is the RC circuit students know: cut-off 1 over 2 pi RC. With 1 kilo-ohm and 1 microfarad that is 159 Hz; ten times higher only a tenth of the amplitude passes. The key point for control: a filter also delays the signal, and delay costs phase.

Emphasise
  • A filter = frequency-selective gain.
  • Misconception: 'a filter only removes noise and has no side effects' — it always adds delay.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Is the RC circuit first or second order? A: First order: one energy store.

Transition: Why would a servo loop need filters at all?

Slide 6Why filters in a servo loop — and where they sit~4 min

We filter sensor noise, resonances, offsets and sudden command steps. The diagram shows four places a filter can sit. The red dotted box marks the loop: anything inside multiplies L(s) and its phase is subtracted from the phase margin. A filter on the reference sits outside: it shapes the response but cannot make the loop unstable.

Emphasise
  • Inside the loop = part of L(s).
  • Misconception: 'filtering the feedback is harmless' — it is inside the loop.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Is a filter on the torque command inside? A: Yes.

Transition: The most common filter: the low-pass.

Slide 7Low-pass filter: cut-off, order and phase~4 min

Cut-off is the -3 dB point. Order sets the slope: minus 20 dB per decade per order. Each order also adds minus 45 degrees of phase at cut-off. The plot compares first, second and fourth order: the higher order cleans better above cut-off but lags far more. Second-order filters with low damping peak, which means they amplify near cut-off.

Emphasise
  • -45° per order at cut-off.
  • Misconception: 'higher order is always better'.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why not always 4th order? A: Three times more phase lag near crossover.

Transition: How much does that phase cost the loop?

Slide 8The price of filtering: phase lost at crossover~5 min

Walk through the worked example step by step. Crossover 150 Hz, we allow 10 degrees. tan 10 degrees is 0.176, so the filter must be at least 150 divided by 0.176, about 850 Hz. Check: arctan of 150 over 850 is 10 degrees. On the plot, find the ratio 5.7 on the first-order curve. Rule of thumb: put the filter 5 to 10 times above crossover. A lower filter makes a quieter motor but less margin.

Emphasise
  • omega_f ≈ 5–10 × omega_c.
  • Misconception: students confuse cut-off of the filter with crossover of the loop.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What if we need a 2nd-order filter? A: It must sit higher, about 1.2 kHz.

Transition: Now the opposite filter: the high-pass.

Slide 9High-pass filter: removing offsets~4 min

A high-pass blocks DC: it removes sensor offsets and drift, like an accelerometer that reads a small constant even at rest. It passes only changes, which is called washout. Below its corner it adds phase lead. The pitfall: never put it in the main position or velocity feedback, because the loop would stop seeing a constant error and could not hold a load against gravity.

Emphasise
  • HPF only for auxiliary signals.
  • Misconception: 'removing DC removes the offset problem everywhere' — it also removes the real steady error.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Example of washout? A: Vibration feedback that must ignore a constant load.

Transition: Next, the most important special filter in servo practice.

Slide 10Notch filter: cutting out one frequency~5 min

Use the playground swing: pushed at its natural frequency it swings higher and higher. A machine axis has such a frequency too. The notch removes the controller's push only there. Three knobs: centre at the resonance, depth set by the ratio of the two dampings, width set by zeta p. Wide notches tolerate a drifting resonance but cost more phase below the centre.

Emphasise
  • Centre, depth, width.
  • Misconception: 'a deeper notch is always better' — width matters more when the resonance drifts.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: How does a drive find the resonance? A: Frequency sweep or FFT of the current.

Transition: Let us see one in action.

Slide 11Notch in practice: taming a 1 kHz resonance~5 min

A motor and load connected by a flexible shaft resonate at 1 kHz. The velocity loop crosses at 100 Hz. Without the notch, the resonance peak pushes loop gain above 0 dB near 1 kHz where there is no phase left, so the loop is unstable: you would hear a loud whistle. With a 25 dB notch, only one crossing remains and the main crossover loses about 4 degrees.

Emphasise
  • The notch keeps the bandwidth; lowering the gain would lose it.
  • Misconception: 'the resonance is outside the bandwidth, so it cannot matter'.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why not just reduce gain? A: You would lose a factor ~3 in gain and bandwidth.

Transition: Filters remove problems; compensators actively add phase.

Slide 12Phase-lead compensator: buying phase back~5 min

A lead places a zero before a pole, so the phase rises between them. The maximum is at the geometric mean of zero and pole; we put that point at crossover. Remember the anchors: alpha 3 gives 30 degrees, 7.5 gives 50, 20 gives 65. The price is more high-frequency gain, so more noise. A lead is just a PD controller with a filtered D.

Emphasise
  • Centre the maximum on crossover.
  • Misconception: 'more alpha is free' — noise gain grows with alpha.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Lead vs D-term? A: Same idea; the pole is the D filter.

Transition: Let us design one together.

Slide 13Lead design — worked example~5 min

A pure inertia has -180 degrees everywhere, so a plain gain gives zero margin. Go through the four steps: we need 50 degrees so alpha is 7.55; centre it with T equal to 1.16 ms; choose K so the loop gain is one at crossover, 7.2 newton-metres per radian; the computed check gives 50 degrees at 49.5 Hz. The 28 percent overshoot comes from the zero; a reference filter removes it.

Emphasise
  • Four steps, units at each step.
  • Misconception: PM 50° does not guarantee a fixed overshoot when zeros are present.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Units of K? A: N·m per rad of position error.

Transition: The opposite compensator: lag.

Slide 14Phase-lag compensator and lead–lag~4 min

A lag boosts gain only at low frequencies, by beta. That makes the axis stiffer against load and reduces steady error, while high frequencies and noise are unchanged. Put its zero a decade below crossover and it costs only about 5 degrees. Lead plus lag together: lead buys margin, lag buys stiffness. PID is exactly that combination.

Emphasise
  • Lag = low-frequency stiffness.
  • Misconception: students think lag 'adds lag to the response' — it hardly touches crossover.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Relation to PI? A: PI is a lag with its pole at zero frequency.

Transition: Summary.

Slide 15Session 2.6 summary and key terms~3 min

Read the three take-aways, then the key terms. Ask students to explain one term to their neighbour in one sentence.

Emphasise
  • Filters cost phase, compensators buy it.

Transition: Quick check.

Slide 16Check your understanding — filters~4 min

Give two minutes in pairs. Answers: (1) a 4th-order filter at only 2× crossover loses well over 45° of phase — the loop becomes badly damped or unstable. (2) F_r is outside the loop and does not change L(s); F_y is inside and its phase adds to L. (3) A notch filter centred at 800 Hz, the whistle frequency, ideally after measuring the resonance.

Emphasise
  • Accept qualitative answers; the point is inside vs outside the loop.

Transition: Break, then state space.

Session 2.7 · Introduction to modern control Curriculum code 2.7 · 60 min · slide times sum to 60 min

Slide 18Refresher: matrices and eigenvalues~7 min

Analogy: a guitar string has natural notes; a system has natural rates, the eigenvalues. Work the example on the board: the determinant gives lambda squared plus 3 lambda plus 2, roots -1 and -2. So the free response contains e to the minus t and e to the minus 2t, both decaying. Eigenvalues in the left half-plane mean stable. Link: eigenvalues of A are the poles from Day 2.

Emphasise
  • Eigenvalues of A = poles.
  • Misconception: 'eigenvectors matter for stability' — only the eigenvalues decide.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What if an eigenvalue is +1? A: e^t grows, unstable.

Transition: Now build a state model of the servo axis.

Slide 19State-space model of a servo axis~6 min

The state is the memory: where the axis is and how fast it moves. Row one says the derivative of angle is speed; row two is Newton's law for rotation. With our numbers, a is 2 per second and b is 1000. Adding motor current gives a third state: same idea, one more row. Each energy store gives one state.

Emphasise
  • One state per energy store.
  • Misconception: the state is not the output; the output is chosen by C.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why two states? A: Speed (kinetic energy in the inertia) and angle (the integral of speed).

Transition: Use the state for feedback.

Slide 20State feedback and pole placement~6 min

Instead of feeding back only the error, we feed back every state with a gain each. Analogy: a driver watching both distance and speed. The closed loop has system matrix A minus BK, so choosing K chooses the poles. The limit: faster poles need more torque and amplify noise.

Emphasise
  • Choose K = choose the poles.
  • Misconception: 'we can make poles arbitrarily fast' — actuators saturate.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What if we cannot measure speed? A: Estimate it: observer.

Transition: Let us place two poles by hand.

Slide 21Worked example: placing the poles~8 min

Four steps on the board. Desired polynomial from omega n 100 and zeta 0.7: s² + 140 s + 10 000. The closed loop gives s² + (2 + 1000 k2) s + 1000 k1. Match the constant terms: k1 = 10. Match the s terms: k2 = 0.138. So k1 acts as a spring and k2 as a damper: this is PD control in disguise.

Emphasise
  • Coefficient matching is enough for 2nd order.
  • Misconception: students mix up which gain sets damping.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What do the observer poles in blue mean? A: Next slide.

Transition: Estimating states we cannot measure.

Slide 22The observer~7 min

Analogy: a navigator without GPS uses speed times time, and corrects when a lighthouse appears. The observer runs a copy of the model and corrects it with the measurement error, weighted by L. The estimation error decays with the poles of A minus LC, which we make 2 to 6 times faster than the controller. Separation principle: design K and L independently.

Emphasise
  • Model + correction.
  • Misconception: 'the observer is a filter' — it uses the model and the input, not just the measurement.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Can we observe angle from speed only? A: No, the starting angle is unknown.

Transition: An example with a real encoder.

Slide 23Observer example: velocity from an encoder~7 min

Only the angle is measured. Differencing gives a staircase because one encoder count per sample equals 0.38 rad/s. The observer gives a smooth estimate that converges in about 10 ms and lags less than differentiate-and-filter because it also uses the torque command.

Emphasise
  • Observer gives smooth speed with little lag.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: What if J is wrong? A: The estimate is biased; check margins.

Transition: What about load torque?

Slide 24Integral action in state feedback~7 min

Add the accumulated error as an extra state, just like the I in PI. With state feedback only, a load of 0.5 N·m against a spring of 10 N·m/rad leaves 0.05 rad error. With the integral state the error returns to zero.

Emphasise
  • Integral state = I action.
  • Misconception: 'state feedback removes all steady error'.

Transition: Some advanced ideas, conceptually.

Slide 25Going further: controllability, Ackermann, LQR~5 min

Conceptual only. Controllable means the input can move every state. Ackermann's formula is what software uses to compute K for bigger systems; for ours it gives the same answer. LQR lets you specify what is expensive, errors or torque, and software returns the best K. No algebra needed.

Emphasise
  • Not examinable; the idea is enough.

Transition: Summary.

Slide 26Session 2.7 summary and key terms~3 min

Read the take-aways; ask a student to define 'observer' in their own words.

Emphasise
  • Poles = eigenvalues; K moves them.

Transition: Check.

Slide 27Check your understanding — state space~4 min

Answers: (1) unstable — the eigenvalue +1 gives a growing e^t. (2) k1 (the spring) for speed via omega n; k2 (the damper) for damping. (3) The estimate must converge before the controller needs it; but too fast amplifies encoder noise and hits sampling limits.

Emphasise
  • Watch for 'more negative = unstable' confusion.

Transition: Break, then adaptive and robust control.

Session 2.8 · Adaptive and robust control Curriculum code 2.8 · 60 min · slide times sum to 60 min

Slide 29Why fixed gains are not enough~8 min

Crane analogy: a driver treats an empty hook and a full load differently. Parameters such as inertia change by 10× in a robot. The plot: a PI tuned for J0 gives 34 % overshoot at 5 J0 and is sluggish at 0.2 J0. Two answers: adapt, or be robust.

Emphasise
  • Crossover ≈ Kp/J moves with J.
  • Misconception: 'tune once and forget'.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Example from your life? A: Car with/without a trailer.

Transition: First answer: robust control.

Slide 30Robust control~9 min

Suspension analogy: one design safe for empty and full car. Uncertainty grows with frequency because we never model all resonances and delays. The plot: closed-loop gain must stay below the grey limit; without the notch it crosses near 1 kHz. Going further, in small text: this is the small-gain test |WT| < 1; H-infinity automates it. Do not derive.

Emphasise
  • Robust = cautious but safe.
  • Misconception: 'robust means optimal' — it trades bandwidth for safety.

Transition: Second answer: change gains with a rule.

Slide 31Gain scheduling~8 min

Gear-change analogy. Tune at a few points, measure a scheduling variable such as robot angle, interpolate. Keep Kp proportional to the estimated inertia so crossover stays fixed. Simple and certifiable, but needs a model and slow variation.

Emphasise
  • No learning: a look-up table.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: Why must rho change slowly? A: Fast changes break the 'frozen' assumption.

Transition: Third answer: let the controller learn.

Slide 32MRAC~10 min

Learner-driver analogy: copy the instructor; every mismatch nudges the technique. The reference model says how we want to respond. The MIT rule changes the gain theta in proportion to error times the model output. The plot: theta converges to 2; larger gamma learns faster, but too large can go unstable.

Emphasise
  • gamma = learning rate.
  • Misconception: 'adaptive always converges to the true parameters' — needs rich excitation.

Transition: Can we prove it is stable?

Slide 33Going further: Lyapunov~6 min

Conceptual. Marble in a bowl: if an energy-like quantity is always positive and always decreasing, the system settles. Lyapunov-based adaptation chooses the learning law so this is guaranteed. No derivation.

Emphasise
  • Stability without solving the ODE.

Transition: A practical robust trick.

Slide 34Disturbance observer~9 min

Compare the torque that should have produced the motion with the torque we commanded; the difference is the disturbance; subtract it. The plot: with P control alone a 0.5 N·m load leaves −0.8 rad/s error; with DOB it returns to zero. Q is a low-pass limited by noise and delay.

Emphasise
  • Common in industrial drives.
  • Misconception: 'DOB needs a perfect model' — only near the Q bandwidth.

Transition: Summary.

Slide 35Session 2.8 summary and key terms~4 min

Walk the table row by row; ask which approach students would use for a robot arm.

Emphasise
  • Industry: scheduling + DOB, rarely full MRAC.

Transition: Check.

Slide 36Check your understanding — adaptive and robust~6 min

Answers: (1) crossover drops by about 3 (ωc ≈ Kp/J): slower, less margin from the PI zero, more overshoot. (2) MRAC learns; scheduling is easier to certify because it is a fixed table. (3) Because unmodelled resonances, delays and current-loop dynamics all live at high frequency.

Emphasise
  • Reward reasoning over formulas.

Transition: Break, then digital control.

Session 2.9 · Discrete-time control Curriculum code 2.9 · 60 min · slide times sum to 60 min

Slide 38Refresher: sampling a signal~7 min

Film-camera analogy: motion shown as snapshots. Sample rate fs = 1/T; at 8 kHz, T is 125 µs and a 100 Hz sine has 80 samples per period. Between samples the output is held: a staircase. On average the staircase follows the signal half a sample late.

Emphasise
  • Holding costs T/2 delay.
  • Misconception: 'samples are exact, so there is no delay'.

Transition: Where sampling sits in a drive.

Slide 39Anatomy of a digital servo loop~5 min

The CPU sees the measurement only at sample instants, computes, and the PWM holds the result. Computing takes time, so the output usually applies one sample later. Table: inner loops sample faster.

Emphasise
  • Typical rates: 8–32 kHz current loop.

Transition: What does the delay cost?

Slide 40Delay budget and phase loss~8 min

Shower analogy: long pipe, late response, overcorrection. Walk the four steps: T = 250 µs, Td = 375 µs, phase loss 360 × 200 × 0.000375 = 27 degrees. Doubling fs halves it.

Emphasise
  • Δφ = 360° fc Td.
  • Misconception: forgetting the computation delay.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: At 16 kHz? A: 6.8°.

Transition: Stability in discrete time.

Slide 41From the s-plane to the z-plane~7 min

A discrete pole multiplies the signal every sample. If its magnitude is below one, the signal shrinks: stable. The unit circle replaces the imaginary axis. Our example pole maps to 0.93: inside, stable, but close to 1 because sampling is fast.

Emphasise
  • |z| < 1 stable.
Ask the audience / likely questions
  • Q: z = −0.9? A: Stable but alternates sign: ringing at fs/2.

Transition: How do we turn C(s) into code?

Slide 42Going further: discretisation and pre-warping~5 min

Conceptual. Different ways to replace s by z; Tustin is the standard. Near half the sample rate it squeezes frequencies, so an 800 Hz notch lands at 714 Hz unless pre-warped. Show the plot, skip algebra.

Emphasise
  • Drives use Tustin with pre-warping.

Transition: The real code.

Slide 43Discrete PID implementation~8 min

Read the code line by line. Integrator: add error times T. D on the measurement to avoid kick. Saturation then anti-windup. Accelerator analogy for windup: keeping the foot down while the car is already at full power, then overshooting.

Emphasise
  • Anti-windup is mandatory.
  • Misconception: 'saturation is rare' — it happens on every big step.

Transition: A sampling trap.

Slide 44Aliasing~6 min

Wagon-wheel effect in films. Anything above fs/2 folds back. 4.6 kHz sampled at 4 kHz looks like 600 Hz. Once sampled you cannot separate it; filter before the sampler.

Emphasise
  • Filter before sampling.

Transition: So how fast should we sample?

Slide 45Choosing the sample rate~6 min

Same PI, only fs changes. At 1 kHz the delay costs 54 degrees and overshoot is 95 %. Rules: 20–40× bandwidth and check the phase budget.

Emphasise
  • Sample rate is a phase decision.

Transition: Summary.

Slide 46Session 2.9 summary and key terms~4 min

Read the take-aways; have students compute the 8 kHz case mentally.

Transition: Check.

Slide 47Check your understanding — discrete-time~4 min

Answers: (1) Td = 1.5/16000 = 94 µs; 360 × 2000 × 94e-6 ≈ 34 degrees — large, that is why current loops run so fast. (2) |z| > 1: the signal grows 5 % every sample: unstable. (3) The output is capped but the error persists, so the integrator keeps growing; when the error reverses it must first unwind, causing overshoot.

Transition: Break, then the servo system.

Slide 54Refresher: diode, capacitor, transistor switch~5 min

Diode: one-way valve. Capacitor: tank, E = ½CV². Transistor: tap opened by a gate. The numbers show why a switch is used only fully on or off: 15 W on, 0 W off, 2.8 kW half-way.

Emphasise
  • On/off only.
  • Misconception: 'a transistor is an adjustable resistor here' — that is a linear amplifier and would burn.

Transition: The whole power stage.

Slide 55Power stage overview~3 min

AC to DC to AC: diodes, pre-charge, capacitor, brake chopper, six-switch inverter.

Emphasise

Transition: The rectifier.

Slide 56Rectifier and DC link~3 min

The diodes pick the highest line voltage; 1.35 × 400 = 540 V; capacitor charges to about 566 V. Pre-charge limits inrush. Diodes cannot return energy.

Transition: So where does braking energy go?

Slide 57Regeneration and brake chopper~4 min

Walk the four steps: 2.47 kJ to dissipate; capacitor holds only 145 J; peak 9.9 kW; resistor 62 Ω max, choose 56 Ω.

Emphasise
  • Capacitor stores only ~6 %.

Transition: Back to the output side.

Slide 58Three-phase inverter~3 min

Each leg ties its output to plus or minus; never both on. Duty 0.75 gives 140 V average.

Transition: Which switches are used?

Slide 59Power switches~2 min

IGBT for 400 V drives, MOSFET for low voltage, SiC for compact fast drives. Faster switching helps control but stresses insulation.

Transition: How do we drive them safely?

Slide 60Gate drivers and dead time~3 min

Gate driver = muscle with isolation and protection. Dead time prevents shoot-through, costing 11.2 V in the example; Day 4 compensates it.

Answers: (1) half-on means large V and I at once: kilowatts of heat. (2) Brake resistor/chopper missing or too small (or deceleration too fast). (3) Current loop first: it is the inner plant of the velocity loop.
